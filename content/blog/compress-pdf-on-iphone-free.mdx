## Does iPhone have a built-in PDF compressor?

Yes, and it's easy to miss. The Files app includes a feature called Optimize File Size:

1. Open the **Files** app on your iPhone.
2. Find your PDF and long-press it.
3. Tap **Quick Actions**, then **Optimize File Size**.

The files app overwrites the original with the smaller version automatically. No extra apps, no account, free.

The catch: you get no say in compression quality, and it doesn't work reliably on every PDF. Many files come back unchanged, especially ones that already contain compressed images or are mostly text. If Optimize File Size does nothing for you, a browser-based tool gives you more control.

## How to compress a PDF in Safari on iPhone (no app, no sign-up)

Our [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) runs entirely in your browser. Your file is processed on your device and never sent to a server.

1. Open **Safari** on your iPhone.
2. Go to the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf).
3. Tap **Choose file** and select your PDF (from Files, iCloud, or any other location).
4. Pick a quality level: **Low** (best quality, moderate size reduction), **Medium**, or **High** (smallest file, most compression).
5. Tap **Compress**, then download the result.

Medium quality is a good starting point for most image-heavy files. If you need the smallest possible file and the visual quality of photos can tolerate more degradation, try High.

## When compression actually helps (and when it makes things worse)

This is the part most guides skip, and it's the most important thing to understand before you compress anything on iPhone.

**Image-heavy PDFs shrink well.** Scanned documents, photo decks, presentations full of photographs, and PDFs exported from image editors all respond well to compression. In our desktop testing, a 6-page image PDF went from 913.4 KB down to 315.3 KB at Medium quality, a 65.5% reduction. That's the kind of PDF browser-based compressors are designed for. Your iPhone results may differ somewhat, but the direction holds: image content compresses.

**Text PDFs get bigger, not smaller.** A 20-page text-only report grew from 23.5 KB to 1.4 MB under the same tool at the same quality level, in the same testing environment. That's not a bug. It's how this type of compression works: the tool converts each page to a [JPEG image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPEG) before saving. Text stored as vector glyphs (which is how [standard PDFs encode text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF#Text)) takes up very little space. The same text captured as a photograph of those glyphs takes much more.

**Mixed PDFs (text with a few images) also get bigger.** A 35 KB document with body text and a small image on each page grew to 171.2 KB at Medium quality.

The rule of thumb: if your PDF contains mainly scans or photos, compression will reduce the size. If it's a word-processed document, an invoice, a contract, or a form, skip the compressor entirely.

## What to do when your PDF won't shrink

A few specific situations and what to try instead:

**PDF is a text document.** If it's too large for email, the problem is usually something else: a high-resolution embedded logo, fonts packaged inside the file, or revision history baked in. Compression won't fix those. Try sending a cloud link instead (via iCloud Drive, Google Drive, or Dropbox) rather than attaching the file.

**Scanned PDF barely shrank.** Switch from Medium to High quality. Or, if you have control over your scanner, rescan at 150 DPI rather than 300 DPI before compression.

**Compressor returned a larger file.** The PDF was probably already compressed, or is text-heavy. Do not run it through a second pass. Applying compression to an already-compressed file tends to increase the size further rather than reduce it.

**File still too large for email.** [Gmail caps attachments at 25 MB](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/6584?hl=en), and other email providers use similar limits. Base64 encoding adds roughly a third to the transmitted size, so the practical safe ceiling is closer to 18 MB. If your PDF is larger than that, even after compression, use a cloud share link instead.

## Tips for sharing a large PDF from iPhone

A few steps that help before reaching for a compressor:

- **Check the actual size first.** In Files, long-press the PDF and tap Info. If it's under 10 MB, most email apps will handle it without trouble.
- **Merge before you compress.** If you're combining several files into one, run them through a [Merge PDF tool](/merge-pdf) first. Merging does not inflate the combined file beyond what the individual parts already add up to. Check the merged size, then decide whether you need to compress at all.
- **Send a link, not an attachment.** For large files, sharing a link via iCloud Drive or Google Drive skips the size limit entirely. This is almost always faster than getting the file small enough to attach.
- **Compress only image-heavy files.** If your PDF has scanned pages or photos, the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) running in Safari is the fastest free option on iPhone. Pick Medium quality and see what the output size is before committing.

The Files app option is there when you want a single tap with no decisions. For more control, or if the built-in option does nothing, the browser-based route in Safari works well on iPhone, keeps your file private, and costs nothing.
