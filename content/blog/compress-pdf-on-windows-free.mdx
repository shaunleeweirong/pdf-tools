Compressing a PDF on Windows for free doesn't require Adobe Acrobat or any software download. Open any browser, go to the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf), pick your file, and get a smaller version back in seconds. Everything runs inside the browser tab, so your document never leaves your PC.

## Does Windows have a built-in PDF compressor?

Not really. Windows 10 and 11 both include Microsoft Print to PDF, which lets you "print" any document to a PDF file. It's not a compressor. It re-renders the document through the printer driver, which sometimes reduces file size and sometimes increases it, with no quality slider and no predictable result.

For a reliable size reduction, a browser-based tool is the better route.

## Which PDFs actually shrink when you compress them?

This is the thing most guides leave out, and it matters a lot.

The compressor works by converting every page to a [JPEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPEG) image at a lower quality setting. That helps enormously when the page is already a scan or a photo, and it makes files larger when the page is mostly text.

In testing:

- A six-page image deck (913.4 KB) compressed to 315.3 KB at Medium quality, a 65.5% reduction.
- The same deck at High quality came out at 175.6 KB, an 80.8% reduction.
- A 20-page text report (23.5 KB) compressed to 1.4 MB at Medium quality. It grew roughly 60 times larger.

The reason is simple. Text stored as vector characters is tiny. A photograph of the same characters is not.

**Compress these:** Scanned pages, photo-heavy presentations, PDFs made from phone camera shots, image decks.

**Skip compression for these:** Invoices, contracts, filled forms, anything mostly made of text. If a text PDF is too large to send, try splitting it with the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf), removing unused pages with the [Delete Pages tool](/delete-pages), or just zipping the file before attaching it.

## How to compress a PDF on Windows step by step

This works on Windows 10 and 11, in any modern browser including Edge, Chrome, and Firefox. No installation and no account needed.

1. Open your browser and go to the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf).
2. Click the upload area, or drag your PDF file from File Explorer onto the page.
3. Choose a quality level. Medium is the right starting point for most files.
4. Click Compress PDF. The progress bar shows compression happening inside your browser tab.
5. When it finishes, click Download. The compressed file saves to your Windows Downloads folder.

From Downloads, you can drag the file straight into Outlook, Gmail, or any messaging app.

## Choosing the right quality level

| Level | What it does | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Low (best quality) | Modest size reduction, very little visible change | Images you plan to print or zoom into closely |
| Medium | Good balance of size and quality | Everyday sharing via email or messaging |
| High (smallest file) | Maximum size reduction, some softness in images | Strict upload limits or slow connections |

Medium is the default for a reason. It brought a 913 KB image deck down to 315 KB in testing, which clears most email size limits. High pushed the same file to 175.6 KB when the smallest possible size was the goal.

## Don't compress a PDF twice on Windows

If the first pass did not shrink the file enough, the natural next step is to run it again. Don't.

A second pass makes the file bigger. The compressor re-renders the already-rasterized pages at a larger internal scale before re-encoding them as JPEG. In testing, a 315 KB file that came out of one Medium pass returned at 599 KB after a second Medium pass, almost double the size.

The right move is to go back to your original file and run it at High instead of Medium. High compression on the original produced 175.6 KB in testing, compared to 315.3 KB at Medium. Starting from the original is always the better path to a smaller output.

## What to do if the file is still too large

Some PDFs won't compress well even at High quality. This happens when:

- The PDF is mostly text (compression makes these larger, see above).
- The images inside are already compressed JPEGs, so re-encoding them saves very little.
- One large image (a high-resolution logo or cover page) accounts for most of the file size.

For these cases, a few other options work well on Windows:

- **Split it.** The [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) pulls out page ranges so you can send parts separately.
- **Remove pages you don't need.** The [Delete Pages tool](/delete-pages) removes specific pages entirely.
- **Zip it.** Right-click the PDF in File Explorer, choose "Compress to ZIP file" (Windows 11) or "Send to > Compressed folder" (Windows 10). Zipping can squeeze out another 10 to 30% on uncompressed content.
- **Send a link instead.** [Gmail caps attachments at 25 MB](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/6584), and Base64 encoding adds about 33% overhead, so the practical ceiling for a PDF attachment is around 18 MB. If the file is close to that, upload it to Google Drive and share a link rather than attaching it directly.
