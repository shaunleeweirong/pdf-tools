## PNG vs JPG: which format should you pick?

When pulling images from a PDF, you have two practical options: PNG and JPG. The right one depends on what's on the page.

**PNG uses [lossless compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lossless_compression).** Every pixel in the output is an exact copy of what was rendered. Nothing is thrown away. That makes PNG the right choice for:

- Documents with body text (reports, contracts, invoices)
- Technical drawings, flowcharts, and diagrams
- Presentation slides
- Anything with sharp edges, thin lines, or logos

**JPG uses lossy compression.** It discards some detail to shrink the file size. For photographs, this trade-off is usually invisible. For text and crisp lines, the loss shows up as faint artifacts, especially at high zoom. JPG makes sense when:

- The page is mostly photographs or scans of photos
- File size matters more than pixel-perfect accuracy

The practical difference is most visible on text. A page of body text as PNG has clean, sharp letters. The same page as JPG has a subtle ringing pattern around each character (called compression artifacts). Usually minor, but it's there.

## Why is the PNG bigger than the original PDF?

This surprises most people. A compact PDF can turn into a much larger PNG, and it's working exactly as intended.

PDF is a vector-first format. Text is stored as font outlines and character coordinates: compact instructions that describe how to draw each letter, not a picture of it. A text-heavy document can fit in a few dozen kilobytes because those instructions compress extremely well.

PNG is a pixel format. When you convert, the renderer draws every character onto a canvas and saves the resulting pixels. Every pixel of every letter on every line gets its own entry in the file. The same content takes far more space stored as pixels than as instructions.

This doesn't mean anything went wrong. PNG is the right format when you need something an image editor, design tool, or website can work with directly. It just stores information differently than PDF does.

Pages that already contain large photographs don't grow as dramatically, because those images were already pixels before the conversion.

## Does PNG keep transparency from the PDF?

[PNG supports an alpha channel](https://www.w3.org/TR/PNG/), a value per pixel that controls opacity. So if your PDF has pages with transparent areas, the PNG output will preserve them.

In practice, most standard PDFs have a solid white background, and the PNG will too. The exception is PDFs exported from design tools like Illustrator or Figma, where pages can have true transparency.

If you need a transparent background and your PDF has a white one, that white is content, not a background. You'd need to remove it manually in an image editor after the conversion.
