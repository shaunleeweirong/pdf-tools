The difference between a digital signature and an electronic signature in a PDF comes down to one thing: cryptography. An electronic signature shows intent. A digital signature adds a mathematical proof of who signed and whether the document has changed since. Both are legally valid in most countries. The question is which one your situation actually requires.

## What is an electronic signature in a PDF?

An electronic signature is any electronic mark that shows you agree to or accept a document. That includes drawing your signature with a mouse or touchscreen, typing your name, checking an "I agree" box, or inserting a scanned image of your handwritten signature.

When you use the [Sign PDF tool](/sign-pdf), you draw your signature and it gets placed directly on the page. That's an electronic signature. It looks like a real signature, it communicates intent, and it's treated as legally binding in most countries.

The [US ESIGN Act](https://www.ftc.gov/tips-advice/business-center/guidance/electronic-signatures-global-and-national-commerce-act), signed in 2000, gives electronic signatures the same legal standing as handwritten ones for most commercial transactions. The EU's [eIDAS Regulation](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eidas-regulation), in effect since 2016, does the same across EU member states. So for a lease, an NDA, a freelance contract, or an internal approval form, a drawn electronic signature is almost always sufficient.

What an electronic signature doesn't do on its own: it doesn't prove the file hasn't been altered after you signed it.

## What is a digital signature in a PDF?

A digital signature is a specific type of electronic signature that uses [public key infrastructure (PKI)](https://csrc.nist.gov/glossary/term/public_key_infrastructure) cryptography. Here is what happens when you apply one:

1. The signing software creates a unique mathematical fingerprint (called a hash) of the entire document at the moment of signing.
2. That hash is encrypted using your private key, which is tied to a certificate issued by a trusted Certificate Authority.
3. The encrypted hash and your certificate get embedded into the PDF file.

When someone opens the signed PDF later, their viewer decrypts the hash using your public key and checks it against the current document. If they match, the signature is valid. If anything in the file was changed after signing, even a single character, the hashes don't match and the signature appears as invalid or tampered.

This tamper-detection capability is what distinguishes a cryptographic digital signature from a drawn one. PDF viewers like Adobe Acrobat Reader show a dedicated "Signatures" panel for PKI-signed documents, listing the signer's name, the Certificate Authority, and the document's integrity status.

## Are both legally binding?

Yes, for most transactions. The ESIGN Act and its equivalents in most countries focus on intent to sign, not the technology behind the signature. A drawn mark and a PKI certificate both satisfy the legal definition of an electronic signature.

The EU's eIDAS regulation is more specific. It defines three tiers:

- **Simple Electronic Signature (SES)**: any electronic sign of intent, such as a drawn or typed signature. Valid for most routine contracts.
- **Advanced Electronic Signature (AES)**: linked to the signer and capable of detecting changes. Requires stronger identity verification.
- **Qualified Electronic Signature (QES)**: created with a certified signing device, using a certificate from a qualified trust provider. QES carries the highest legal weight in the EU and is treated as equivalent to a handwritten signature in court.

For most people signing everyday documents, a Simple Electronic Signature (a drawn or typed mark) is enough. QES comes up for notarized documents, certain cross-border EU filings, or regulated sectors in specific countries.

## When does a drawn signature fall short?

A drawn electronic signature is the right choice for:

- Freelance contracts and service agreements
- NDAs and confidentiality agreements
- Employment offer letters
- Lease agreements in most jurisdictions
- Internal approvals and sign-offs
- Online purchase agreements

You are more likely to need a cryptographic digital signature when:

- You are submitting regulated filings (financial reports, healthcare records, certain government forms)
- A transaction crosses EU borders and the counterparty requires AES or QES by law or policy
- You need independently verifiable proof the document has not been modified, for instance in a legal dispute or external audit
- Your industry's compliance framework mandates it (some financial and healthcare regulations do)

One practical note: when someone says "I need a digital signature," they often just mean they want you to sign electronically. Ask whether they need a PKI certificate embedded in the file or whether a drawn signature on the document is what they're after. The two are very different technically, and the distinction matters.

## What if the person asking for your signature needs identity verification?

A drawn electronic signature doesn't include cryptographic identity verification on its own. If the counterparty requires verified identity (a bank, a government agency, or a service like DocuSign or Adobe Sign), they add that layer on their end, typically through email confirmation, SMS codes, or a formal digital certificate flow.

If you specifically need a PKI digital certificate embedded in a PDF, you'll need dedicated software such as Adobe Acrobat Pro or a qualified trust service provider. That is outside what a browser-based drawing tool does, and any tool that claims otherwise should be examined carefully.

For the vast majority of people signing leases, contracts, consent forms, and work agreements, a free drawn electronic signature is exactly what is needed. It is legally binding, takes under a minute, and your file never leaves your device.
