PDF vs JPEG for printing sounds like a simple question, but the answer depends on what you are printing and where. For documents with text and layout, PDF is almost always the right call. For photos going to a lab or kiosk, a high-resolution JPEG is often exactly what the service expects.

## PDF wins for documents and layouts

If your file has text, a specific page design, or multiple pages, use PDF.

PDF stores text as outlines or embeds the fonts used in the document. That means a headline prints at crisp, sharp quality regardless of the printer's resolution. With a JPEG, text is flattened into pixels. Look at a JPEG with text at full resolution and you'll see soft edges around every letter; print it large enough and the blurring becomes visible.

PDF also keeps your layout exactly where you put it. Margins, column widths, header positions, and image placement stay fixed. The file reproduces the same way on any printer anywhere. A JPEG is a flat image with no concept of page layout or typography.

The [PDF format is defined by ISO 32000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF) and is the standard for print-ready documents in publishing, legal, and business. If you need to send a contract, report, brochure, or presentation to a printer, a PDF is the file type they expect.

Multi-page documents are another clear win for PDF. A single JPEG holds one image. If you want to send a 10-page document to a print shop, you need a PDF.

## JPEG can work well for photo prints

For single photos, JPEG is often the right call, because photo labs and kiosks are built around it.

The key number is 300 DPI (dots per inch) at the intended print size. A 4x6 inch photo needs at least 1200x1800 pixels. An 8x10 print needs at least 2400x3000 pixels. If your JPEG meets that threshold and was saved at maximum quality (minimum compression), it will print sharply.

JPEG uses [lossy compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lossy_compression), which means it removes some image data to shrink the file. The effect is invisible at high-quality settings. But it adds up over multiple saves. Re-saving a JPEG before printing compounds the quality loss, so always print from the best copy available, ideally straight from the camera or scanner.

Most consumer photo services (photo books, canvas prints, photo lab kiosks) accept JPEG files and handle the print-specific settings on their end.

## Why print shops prefer PDF

Commercial printers and copy shops almost always request PDF, for two main reasons.

First, PDF supports vector graphics. A company logo stored as a vector inside a PDF prints at the printer's native resolution, which is typically 1200 to 2400 DPI on a laser printer or higher on a commercial press. That same logo converted to JPEG is limited to whatever pixel resolution it was saved at.

Second, professional print PDFs follow the [PDF/X standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF/X), a subset of PDF designed for reliable, predictable printing. PDF/X files embed all fonts, declare a color profile, and include bleed and trim marks. Sending a PDF/X file to a printer removes most of the guesswork on their end.

If you have a PDF and need to supply it to a service that only accepts images, use the [PDF to JPG tool](/pdf-to-jpg) to convert each page. Just be aware that the vector sharpness is lost in that conversion.

## When JPEG is the better option

There are real situations where JPEG is clearly the right choice.

At a photo kiosk (in a pharmacy or supermarket), you upload JPEG files. These machines are designed for it, and sending a PDF may fail or produce unexpected results.

Online photo book services work the same way. You upload individual JPEG images and their software assembles the layout. Trying to upload a multi-page PDF usually does not work.

If you need to send someone a single photo for them to print themselves, JPEG is universal. Every device and operating system can open a JPEG without any special software.

## How to convert between formats before printing

If you have a PDF and need to upload it to a photo lab, convert each page to a JPEG using the [PDF to JPG tool](/pdf-to-jpg). You get one JPEG per page, ready to upload to any photo service.

Going the other way, if you have a set of JPEG photos and want to send them as a single PDF to a print shop, the [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf) combines them into one document. This is also handy for submitting multiple scanned pages or a set of photos as one file.

If your PDF is large and you want to reduce it before uploading to a print service, the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) can help, but only for image-heavy files. The tool rasterizes pages to JPEG internally, which shrinks photo-heavy PDFs but makes text-only PDFs significantly larger. If your PDF is mostly text, do not compress it before printing; it will end up bigger, not smaller.

## A quick comparison

| | PDF | JPEG |
|---|---|---|
| Text quality | Sharp at any size | Softens when zoomed |
| Photos | Works well | Works well at 300 DPI |
| Multiple pages | Yes | No (one image per file) |
| Print shops | Preferred | Usually not accepted |
| Photo kiosks | Usually not accepted | Preferred |
| Re-save quality loss | None | Yes (gets worse each save) |

For a document, report, or anything with text: use PDF. For a single photo at a kiosk or online photo service: JPEG is fine, as long as the resolution is high enough for the print size.
