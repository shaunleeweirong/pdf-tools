You can password protect a PDF on Windows 10 for free in under a minute, without installing Adobe Acrobat. The three practical options are a browser-based tool, Microsoft Word (if you have it), or LibreOffice.

## Does Windows 10 have a built-in PDF password tool?

No. Microsoft Edge can open and print PDFs natively, but it cannot encrypt them with a password. Windows also includes a "Print to PDF" printer that creates PDFs from other documents, but it adds no protection. For a password on a PDF, you need one of the methods below.

## Method 1: Password protect a PDF in your browser (no install)

The quickest option on any Windows version is a browser-based tool. Our [Protect PDF tool](/protect-pdf) runs the encryption entirely inside your browser using [AES-256](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Encryption_Standard), the same cipher trusted by governments and financial institutions. Your file never travels to a server, so nothing is stored or shared. No account needed.

Here is how to do it:

1. Open [Protect PDF](/protect-pdf) in any browser on your Windows 10 PC.
2. Click **Choose file** (or drag your PDF onto the page).
3. Type a strong password in the field provided. Use at least 12 characters, mixing letters, numbers, and at least one symbol.
4. Click **Protect PDF**.
5. Download the encrypted file when it appears.

Anyone who opens that PDF in Adobe Reader, Microsoft Edge, Chrome, or any standard viewer will be prompted for the password before they can read a single page.

The size change is minimal. Encryption adds roughly 1 KB of overhead, so a 913 KB image-heavy PDF comes out at about 914 KB after protection, and a 23 KB text report comes out at about 24 KB. That overhead won't push a file over an email attachment limit.

One important thing: write your password down somewhere safe before you close the browser tab. There is no recovery option on an encrypted PDF. If you forget the password, the file is effectively locked for good.

## Method 2: Use Microsoft Word

If Microsoft Word is already installed on your Windows 10 PC, you can encrypt a PDF during the save step, with no extra software. This method works best when you already have the source document in Word.

1. Open Word and go to **File > Open**, then select your PDF. Word converts it to an editable document.
2. Go to **File > Export > Create PDF/XPS**.
3. In the dialog box, click **Options**.
4. Check **Encrypt the document with a password**.
5. Enter your password, confirm it, then click **OK**.
6. Click **Publish**.

Word's PDF conversion can reflow text in complex layouts, so check the output carefully if the original PDF has precise formatting or tables. For a simple text document this usually works fine.

## Method 3: LibreOffice (free, offline, open source)

[LibreOffice](https://www.libreoffice.org/) is a free, open-source office suite for Windows, macOS, and Linux. Its Draw application can open existing PDFs and export them back with a password. It works entirely offline, which makes it a solid choice for sensitive documents on machines without reliable internet access.

1. Download and install LibreOffice from libreoffice.org (free, no account needed).
2. Open your PDF in LibreOffice Draw: **File > Open**, select the PDF.
3. Go to **File > Export as PDF**.
4. In the PDF Options dialog, click the **Security** tab.
5. Set an **Open Password** so a viewer must enter it to open the document.
6. Click **Export**, choose a filename and location, then click **Save**.

LibreOffice applies AES-256 encryption to the output, the same level as commercial tools. The interface is less polished than a dedicated PDF app, but it gets the job done and costs nothing.

## How strong is AES-256 PDF encryption?

AES-256 is the current standard for PDF encryption, codified in [ISO 32000-2](https://www.iso.org/standard/75839.html) (the PDF 2.0 specification). It uses a 256-bit key and puts data through 14 rounds of mathematical transformations. Trying every possible key combination would take far longer than the age of the universe on current hardware.

The practical weakness is almost always the password, not the cipher. A password like "password1" falls quickly to a dictionary attack. A password like "7kQp!xL4vNm2" is a different story entirely. Use a password manager to generate and store a strong one.

Also worth noting: password protection locks the content but does not hide the file's existence. The file name and its size remain visible to anyone who has it. If you need the file itself to stay hidden, that is a separate problem requiring a different solution.

## Should you compress the PDF before protecting it?

If your PDF is large and you plan to email it after protecting it, consider [compressing it first](/compress-pdf), then adding the password. Compression on an image-heavy PDF can cut the size substantially before you add the password layer.

Be careful about what type of PDF you compress, though. Compression works well on PDFs that contain photos or scanned images. If the document is mostly text, such as a contract, invoice, or typed report, the compressor will actually make it larger, so skip that step and send the original size. Once a PDF is encrypted, do not try to compress it. The compressor cannot read through the encryption to process the page contents.
