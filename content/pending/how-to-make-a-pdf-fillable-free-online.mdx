You can make any PDF fillable free online by adding interactive form fields to it. Open a free browser tool, upload your PDF, click to place text boxes or checkboxes where you need them, and download the result. No Adobe Acrobat required, and no sign-up for the best options.

This guide explains what making a PDF fillable actually involves, which free tools work best, how to handle sensitive documents, and what to do once the form is ready to fill.

## What "making a PDF fillable" actually means

A fillable PDF contains interactive form fields defined in the [PDF specification (ISO 32000)](https://www.iso.org/standard/75839.html). These fields have types (text, checkbox, radio button, dropdown, signature) and names. Every compliant PDF viewer, including Adobe Reader, Preview on Mac, Chrome's built-in viewer, and Edge, can render them so users can click and type.

A regular PDF is flat. The text and layout are locked. You can type on top of it with a PDF edit tool, but that's different from having proper form fields. Overlaid text doesn't behave like a field. It won't tab between entries, won't validate input, and won't export to a data format.

When someone says they want to "make a PDF fillable," they usually mean they have a form template (an invoice, a questionnaire, an application) and want recipients to fill it in digitally without printing and scanning. Adding real form fields is the right solution.

## Free tools that add form fields (no sign-up)

Two online options stand out for being fully free with no account required:

**PDF24 Form Creator** (tools.pdf24.org) is the most capable free option. Upload your PDF, then click anywhere on any page to place a field. You can add text boxes, checkboxes, radio button groups, dropdowns, list boxes, and signature areas. Set each field's name, font size, and whether it is required. Download the finished PDF when done. No limits on file count, no watermark, no registration.

**Sejda PDF Forms** (sejda.com) has a similar workflow with a slightly cleaner interface. The free tier has a cap on the number of tasks per hour and a file size ceiling, but it handles most standard form-creation jobs. No sign-up is needed.

Both tools produce a standard PDF with proper [AcroForm fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF#Interactive_elements), which any PDF viewer can read.

## Types of fields you can add

- **Text field**: A box where someone types a short answer, like a name, address, or date.
- **Multiline text field**: For longer free-text responses, like a description or comments section.
- **Checkbox**: A single tick box for an independent yes/no choice.
- **Radio button group**: A set of options where only one can be selected at a time. Good for "choose your plan" or "select your gender" fields.
- **Dropdown list**: A compact menu that expands to show all choices. Good for lists with many options.
- **Signature field**: A defined area for a drawn or typed signature.

Most free tools support all of these. Signature fields sometimes behave differently depending on the viewer, so test them after creating the form.

## When privacy matters: tools that keep your file local

Some PDFs shouldn't leave your device. Legal contracts, medical intake forms, tax documents, financial statements. For these, you need a tool that processes the file in your browser without uploading it to any server.

**fillPDF.app** runs entirely client-side. Nothing is sent to a server. It's free, requires no account, and adds no watermark.

**PDFgear** (desktop app, free) processes files locally on your machine. It has a broader feature set than most browser tools, including the ability to auto-detect where fields might go based on the visual layout.

**LibreOffice Draw** (free, open-source, runs on Windows, Mac, and Linux) can open PDFs and add form controls through its built-in form toolbar. Every step happens on your computer. It's slower to learn than a web tool, but it has no file-size limits and works offline.

For anything sensitive, the extra few minutes to use a local tool is worth it.

## Step-by-step: add form fields with PDF24

1. Go to **tools.pdf24.org** and choose "Create Fillable PDF Form."
2. Upload your PDF.
3. In the toolbar at the top, select the field type you want (text field, checkbox, etc.).
4. Click on the page where you want the field. Drag to set the size.
5. In the properties panel, give the field a name. Names like "first_name" and "date_of_birth" make it easy to identify fields later.
6. Repeat for every field you need.
7. Click **Download** to save the finished PDF.

Before you send the form to anyone, open the downloaded file in your browser and click through every field to confirm it works.

## What if the PDF is a scanned image?

A scanned document is a photograph of paper, not a structured PDF with text layers. You can't add form fields to a scan the same way you'd add them to a typed PDF, because there's no underlying layout structure to anchor the fields to.

Your options:

1. **Overlay fields manually.** Use one of the tools above to place text field overlays roughly where the blanks appear in the scanned image. It works, but alignment requires careful clicking and the form will look slightly off if the scan is rotated or skewed.
2. **Recreate the form as a typed document.** Open a word processor, type out the form's text, export as PDF, then add proper form fields. More work upfront, but the result is a clean, properly structured form.
3. **Run OCR first.** An [OCR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_character_recognition) tool converts the scan into a text-based PDF. From there, you can add form fields normally. Quality depends on how clean the original scan is.

For a one-off job, option 1 is fastest. For a form you'll use repeatedly, option 2 gives you better long-term results.

## Fillable vs editable: not the same thing

People sometimes confuse "fillable" with "editable." They're different:

- **Fillable PDF**: Has form fields in defined locations. Users fill in those slots. The rest of the document text is locked and can't be changed.
- **Editable PDF**: The actual text and layout can be modified directly, the same way you'd edit a Word document.

Most people who want a "fillable" form actually want the first option. They want to distribute a document where others can enter their information without being able to rewrite the form's instructions or headings.

Adding form fields with PDF24 or Sejda gives you the fillable version. To edit the actual text content of a PDF, you'd need a different approach entirely, typically converting the PDF back to a Word or text document first, making your changes, and re-exporting.
