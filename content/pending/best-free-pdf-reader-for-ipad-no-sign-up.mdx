The best free PDF reader for iPad with no sign-up is one you already have: the **Files app** that ships with every iPad. Tap any PDF in Files and iPadOS opens it right away, no account, no download, nothing extra. If you want to go further, whether that means annotation, Apple Pencil markup, or a proper library view, read on.

## Does your iPad already have a PDF reader?

It does. Every iPad running iPadOS 11 or later ships with two apps that read PDFs out of the box.

**Files app.** This is the quickest way to open a PDF. Tap the file and a full-screen viewer opens with smooth scrolling, pinch-to-zoom, and a search bar. From the share sheet you can highlight, draw, or add a typed note using the built-in [Markup tool](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102462). No account, no subscription.

**Apple Books.** Drag or share a PDF into Books and it sits in your library alongside ebooks. The reading experience is clean, and Books remembers your last page and any highlights across your Apple devices if you use iCloud. Again, no separate account is needed beyond your Apple ID.

For reading only, both of these are good enough that most people don't need to install anything else.

## When you need more: the best third-party apps with no account

Some tasks, like filling a form, comparing two documents, or using split-screen on iPad, need a dedicated app. Here are the best options that don't gate everything behind a paywall or account wall.

**Xodo.** Xodo is fully free and doesn't require an account. You get highlighting, freehand drawing, sticky notes, text comments, form filling, and split-screen comparison all without paying or registering. It syncs with Google Drive and Dropbox if you choose to connect those, but it works fine offline with local files too. Xodo is the strongest no-account option for annotation-heavy work.

**Apple Books (for a library).** Mentioned above, but worth calling out again: if you just want a clean reader with a bookshelf and cross-device sync, Books is hard to beat and genuinely free.

**PDF Expert (free reading tier).** Readdle's PDF Expert has a generous free tier that covers reading, basic highlighting, and navigation. Editing text and other advanced features require a paid plan. No account is required to use the free reading features. Readdle is a well-regarded iOS developer and the app is fast on modern iPads.

**GoodReader.** GoodReader costs a small one-time fee but has been a standby for professionals and students since 2009 because it handles very large files and complex folder structures well. Not free, but mentioning it here because it comes up in every honest comparison and the one-time price makes it cheaper long-term than subscriptions.

## Which apps require a sign-up (and whether that's a dealbreaker)

**Adobe Acrobat Reader.** Acrobat Reader for iPad is free in the App Store but asks you to create a free Adobe account before you can open files. For basic reading, it is solid; the app has good rendering quality and supports most PDF features. The sign-up requirement is the main drawback if you're trying to avoid creating yet another account.

**PDFelement by Wondershare.** Free to download, but Wondershare requires a Wondershare ID to access most features. If you're willing to register, it's a capable option. If not, Xodo does more for free without the account step.

**UPDF.** UPDF has a free tier but pushes you to register to use syncing and some annotation tools. The reading itself is fine.

## What to use with Apple Pencil

If you have an Apple Pencil, the cheapest path is the Markup tool built into Files. It supports pressure sensitivity with the Pencil and lets you draw, highlight, add shapes, and type notes. For students or anyone doing light markup, it's enough.

For serious annotation, handwriting-to-text, or sketching in the margins, Xodo handles Apple Pencil input well and still costs nothing. PDF Expert is another good pick; the free tier supports Pencil-based annotation in its reading mode.

## How to prep your PDFs before reading on iPad

A few things make a PDF much better to read on a small-ish screen.

**Shrink large files first.** If you've got a PDF full of scanned pages or photos, [compress it](/compress-pdf) in your browser before moving it to your iPad. The tool reduces file size by rasterizing pages to JPEG, which works well for image-heavy documents and keeps files small for easier storage. Don't use it on text-only PDFs like contracts or invoices, as those will actually get larger.

**Merge documents before reading.** If you're working from several separate PDFs (say, chapters of a textbook, or a set of reports), [merge them](/merge-pdf) into a single file first. A single PDF is easier to manage in Files or Xodo than six separate ones.

**Split out just what you need.** If you only need a few pages of a long document, [split the PDF](/split-pdf) and copy just those pages to your iPad. Smaller files open faster and take less storage.

The short version: start with the Files app. If you need annotation, get Xodo. If you need to prep a PDF first, do it in your browser before it ever touches your iPad.

Apple's [support page on using PDFs in iPadOS](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102462) covers the built-in Markup features in detail, including which tools work with and without an Apple Pencil. The [PDF format specification (ISO 32000)](https://www.iso.org/standard/75839.html) explains why rendering quality can differ between apps: each reader implements the spec slightly differently, which is why a PDF that looks great in one app may show a slightly different layout in another.
