Adding a digital signature to a PDF is something most people can do free, right in their browser, in under a minute. If you've been searching for this, you probably just want to put your signature on a document and send it back. This guide shows you how to do that, then explains when the technical distinction between "electronic" and "digital" signatures actually matters.

## The quick answer: how to sign a PDF free online

Use the [Sign PDF tool](/sign-pdf). It runs in your browser, needs no account, and the PDF stays on your device.

**Steps:**

1. Go to the [Sign PDF tool](/sign-pdf).
2. Click to open your PDF, or drag and drop it onto the page.
3. Click "Add signature." You'll see three options: draw your signature with your mouse or finger, type your name, or upload an image of your handwritten signature.
4. Click anywhere on the page to place the signature. Drag it to the right spot.
5. Resize using the corner handle if needed.
6. Download the signed PDF.

That covers the vast majority of signing situations: contracts, rental agreements, offer letters, forms, NDAs. The signature becomes part of the PDF and shows up wherever you placed it.

## What people usually mean by "digital signature"

Search results for "digital signature" mix two very different things, and it causes a lot of confusion.

In everyday language, "digital signature" just means "a signature on a digital document." That's what most people are looking for, and the Sign PDF tool handles it.

In technical and legal language, a digital signature is something specific: a signature that uses a [public key infrastructure (PKI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_key_infrastructure) certificate to cryptographically bind your identity to the document. It works by creating a mathematical fingerprint of the document at the moment you sign. If anyone edits the PDF afterward, the fingerprint changes and the signature shows as invalid in PDF viewers.

You only need the cryptographic kind in specific high-stakes situations, which are covered below. For most business and personal documents, an electronic signature (draw, type, or upload) is all you need and is fully legal.

## Are electronic signatures legally binding?

Yes, in most countries. In the United States, the [E-SIGN Act (15 U.S.C. § 7001)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-114/pdf/STATUTE-114-Pg464.pdf) has given electronic signatures the same legal weight as handwritten ones since 2000. The [EU's eIDAS Regulation](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eidas-regulation) does the same across EU member states, with different tiers of assurance.

There are exceptions where a wet (handwritten) signature or notarization is still required:

- Wills and testamentary trusts (in most US states)
- Some real estate deeds, depending on jurisdiction
- Adoption papers and family court documents
- Certain government filings that explicitly require notarization

For everything else, an electronic signature is the standard. Courts routinely accept electronically signed contracts, and most businesses have moved entirely to e-signatures for routine documents.

## When you actually need a cryptographic digital signature

A PKI-based digital signature is worth the extra setup if:

- The recipient or a system needs to verify your identity automatically, without you being present.
- The document must show evidence of any tampering after signing.
- A regulation specifically requires a "qualified electronic signature," such as [FDA 21 CFR Part 11](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/part-11-electronic-records-electronic-signatures-scope-and-application) for pharmaceutical companies.
- You're filing with a government agency or court that requires certified or notarized digital signatures.

For these cases, platforms like DocuSign, Adobe Sign, or government PKI systems provide certificate-backed signatures. These are usually subscription services, not free.

The free in-browser approach is the right tool for rental agreements, service contracts, consent forms, job offers, client approvals, and similar everyday documents. It is not the right tool if a contract clause specifically requires a "certified digital signature" or if you're filing court documents that mandate notarization.

## How to draw a good signature

If you're drawing your signature with a mouse, it can come out looking scratchy. A few tips:

- Use a touchscreen or drawing tablet if you have one. A stylus produces a much cleaner result.
- Draw slowly and deliberately. Speed creates jagged curves.
- If it comes out wrong, click the clear button and try again before placing it.

Alternatively, upload an image. Sign your name with a pen on white paper, photograph or scan it, crop it tightly, and upload it to the tool. This gives you the same appearance as your handwritten signature with none of the mouse-drawing awkwardness.

## Signing PDFs on a phone or tablet

Browser-based signing works well on mobile. Open the tool in your phone's browser, load your PDF, and draw your signature with your finger. Touchscreens make this easier than a mouse because you can control the stroke directly.

If you need to sign multiple pages or several documents, keep the tool open in a browser tab. You can load each PDF one at a time and download the signed versions.

## Can I add multiple signatures or initials?

Yes. After placing your first signature, you can add it again to another location on the same page. If the document asks for both a full signature and initials, create two separate signature entries: one with your full name, one with just your initials.

## What about timestamp and audit trail?

A signature drawn or typed in a browser tool is embedded in the PDF as an image. It doesn't include a timestamp or audit trail by default. If you need a documented audit trail (who signed, what IP address, at what time), you need a dedicated e-signature platform rather than a simple sign tool.

For contracts where both parties need a verifiable record of when and by whom the document was signed, consider platforms built for that purpose. For internal documents, informal approvals, and low-stakes agreements, a signed PDF from a browser tool is fine.
