Compressing a PDF for Dropbox upload is the fastest way to free up storage on a free account and keep shared links opening quickly. The whole process takes under a minute, runs in your browser, and doesn't require a sign-up.

## Why compress a PDF before uploading to Dropbox

[Dropbox Basic](https://www.dropbox.com/basic) (the free plan) gives you 2 GB of total storage. That's the lowest free quota among major cloud storage services, and it fills up quickly once you start storing PDFs: a scan, a presentation deck, a client portfolio.

Dropbox does not compress or reduce file sizes when you upload. [According to Dropbox's own help documentation](https://help.dropbox.com/sync/upload-limitations), files are stored exactly as you upload them. Whatever size you hand over is the size Dropbox keeps. So if you want a smaller file in your account, you have to reduce it before it goes up.

Smaller PDFs also sync faster across devices and open more quickly when a colleague clicks a shared link. If you're sending a Dropbox link over a slow connection, a 175 KB PDF beats a 913 KB one every time.

## What kinds of PDFs compress well

This is the most important thing to know before you compress anything: the tool rasterizes every page to JPEG. That works extremely well when pages already contain images, and it works poorly when pages are mostly text.

**Image-heavy PDFs** (photo portfolios, scanned documents, presentation decks packed with graphics) compress well. In testing, a six-page image deck went from 913.4 KB down to 315.3 KB at Medium and 175.6 KB at High compression.

**Text-only PDFs** (invoices, contracts, spreadsheet exports, typed reports) get larger when you compress them. A 20-page text report went from 23.5 KB to 1.4 MB. If your PDF is mostly text, skip compression entirely. Try the [Split PDF](/split-pdf) tool to break the document into smaller sections, or zip the file before uploading.

**Mixed PDFs** (some text, some images) tend to grow too. The compression savings only apply to pages that are already rasterized images. If you're unsure what's in your file, compress it, then check the resulting size before uploading. If it's bigger, it's a text-heavy document.

## How to compress a PDF for Dropbox

1. Open the [Compress PDF](/compress-pdf) tool in your browser. No account or download needed.
2. Click "Select PDF" and choose your file. The file stays in your browser and never gets sent to a server.
3. Choose a compression level. Medium is a reasonable starting point for most image-heavy files.
4. Click Compress and wait a few seconds.
5. Download the compressed file.
6. Check the resulting file size. If it's smaller than the original, upload it to Dropbox. If it's bigger, your PDF is likely text-heavy. Use Split PDF or a zip file instead.

## Which compression level to choose

There are three levels, from lightest to most aggressive:

**Low (best quality)** takes off the smallest amount. The same image deck dropped from 913.4 KB to 703.5 KB (about 23% smaller). Use this when quality is the priority and you just need a slight size reduction.

**Medium** is the right default for most cases. The image deck went from 913.4 KB to 315.3 KB, a 65.5% reduction. Most shared links and Dropbox previews look fine at Medium.

**High (smallest file)** gives the biggest reduction. The same deck dropped to 175.6 KB, about 80% smaller than the original. Images compressed at High will look slightly softer at 100% zoom, but for typical Dropbox sharing purposes the quality is usually acceptable.

One rule matters here: pick the right level the first time and start from the original file. Running the already-compressed output through the tool again at the same level makes the file bigger, not smaller. A second pass at Medium turned a 315.3 KB file into a 598.9 KB file in testing. If Medium isn't small enough, start fresh from the original and choose High.

## What to do if compression doesn't help

If your PDF is mostly text, compression increases the file size. Here are the alternatives:

**Split the PDF.** If you only need part of the document in Dropbox, use the [Split PDF](/split-pdf) tool to pull out just the pages you need. A 50-page report broken into five 10-page chunks is much easier to store and share.

**Zip the file.** Compressing a text PDF as a ZIP archive uses a different algorithm (deflate rather than JPEG) and can meaningfully shrink text documents. On macOS, right-click the file and choose "Compress." On Windows, right-click and choose "Send to > Compressed (zipped) folder." Upload the ZIP to Dropbox.

**Check your Dropbox quota.** If you're consistently hitting the [2 GB Dropbox Basic limit](https://help.dropbox.com/storage-space/over-quota), compressing a handful of files might not be enough long-term. Removing files you no longer need, or upgrading to a paid plan, may be a better solution.

## A note on file privacy

The Compress PDF tool runs entirely in your browser. Your PDF is never uploaded to a server, which matters if you're compressing contracts, client documents, or anything sensitive before putting it in Dropbox. The file goes directly from your device to the compressed download, with nothing in between.

This makes it a good fit for the kinds of files people typically store in Dropbox for work: agreements, reports, invoices, and presentation decks. Compress the image-heavy ones, skip compression for the text-only ones, and your Dropbox storage will go a lot further.
