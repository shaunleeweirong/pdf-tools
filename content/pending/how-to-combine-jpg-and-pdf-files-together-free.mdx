You can combine JPG and PDF files together free in two browser steps: convert the photos to a PDF first, then merge everything into one document. No account needed, no software to install, and your files never leave your device.

This works for any situation where you need images and a PDF in the same file. Attaching photos of supporting documents to an application, adding scanned receipts to an expense report, or appending product images to a written proposal all follow the same two-step process.

## Why JPGs and PDFs need a two-step process

[JPEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPEG) and [PDF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF) are different formats. A JPEG is a compressed image file. A PDF is a structured container that holds text, fonts, images, and sometimes interactive form fields. Most merge tools only accept PDFs as input, so you can't drag a JPEG straight into a PDF combiner.

The fix is straightforward: convert the JPEG files into a PDF first, then merge that PDF with your existing document. The conversion step places each photo on its own page. The merge step joins those pages with your original PDF in whatever order you choose.

## How to combine JPG and PDF files free (step by step)

### Step 1: Convert your photos to a PDF

1. Open the [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf).
2. Click the upload area and select your JPG files. You can add multiple photos at once.
3. Arrange the images in the order you want them to appear in the final document. Each photo becomes one page.
4. Click **Convert** and download the resulting PDF.

If you have three photos, you get a three-page PDF. If the photos are already in the right sequence in your file browser, they'll come through in that order.

### Step 2: Merge with your existing PDF

1. Open the [Merge PDF tool](/merge-pdf).
2. Upload your original PDF and the image PDF you just created.
3. Drag the file tiles to set the final page order. If the photos should go at the end, place the image PDF second. If they're a cover, place them first.
4. Click **Merge** and download the combined file.

The output contains all the pages from both files in the order you set.

## Does the order of files matter?

Yes. The tile arrangement in the Merge PDF tool determines the page order in the output. Take a moment to get it right before you click Merge.

For a typical workflow like adding photos to the end of a report, upload the report PDF first and the image PDF second. For a portfolio where photos lead, reverse the order. You can drag tiles freely before you commit, so there's no guessing.

## How much will the combined file size increase?

Adding pages always increases the total size, but the merge step itself adds almost no overhead. In testing with our [Merge PDF](/merge-pdf) tool, three files totalling 971.9 KB combined into an output of 970.5 KB, marginally smaller than the sum of the parts because shared PDF structure is only written once.

Expect the combined file to be close to the sum of your inputs. A 1.5 MB PDF and a 2 MB image PDF will produce roughly a 3.5 MB result.

If that's too large to send by email or upload somewhere, compress the image PDF before merging. The [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) is designed for image-heavy files. In testing, a six-page image deck went from 913.4 KB down to 175.6 KB at the High setting. Compress the image PDF first, then merge the compressed version with your original.

## What if your PDF has a form you've filled in?

This is a detail worth knowing before you start, especially if you're combining a filled application or contract with supporting photos.

The merge step copies page content but doesn't carry over the document's interactive form layer. If your PDF has form fields (text boxes, checkboxes, dropdown menus), those fields won't exist in the merged output. The typed values may still appear as static content depending on how the form was built, but the interactive fields themselves are removed.

The fix: flatten the PDF before merging. The [Flatten PDF tool](/flatten-pdf) converts every field value into permanent page content, so it survives the merge intact. The resulting page looks exactly like your filled-in form but without the editable layer.

When your PDF has a filled form, here's the full sequence:

1. Open [Flatten PDF](/flatten-pdf), upload the filled form, and download the flattened version.
2. Open [Images to PDF](/jpg-to-pdf), add your photos, and download the image PDF.
3. Open [Merge PDF](/merge-pdf), upload the flattened PDF and the image PDF, arrange the order, and download.

If your PDF is a scanned document (photographed on a phone, faxed, or photocopied), skip the flatten step. Scans are just images inside a PDF container and have no interactive fields to worry about.

## Do files get uploaded to a server?

No. Both the [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf) and the [Merge PDF tool](/merge-pdf) run entirely in your browser using JavaScript. Your photos and documents are processed locally on your device and never transmitted to a server.

This matters when you're working with anything sensitive: identity documents, signed contracts, medical records, or financial paperwork. The files stay on your machine throughout the entire process.

A few situations where combining JPGs and a PDF in one document is genuinely useful:

- Attaching photos of an ID or supporting document to a PDF application form
- Adding a photo of a signed page to a digital contract (useful when only one page needed a physical signature)
- Combining scanned receipts with a PDF expense report
- Appending screenshots or product images to a written report or proposal
- Creating a portfolio PDF where designed pages are followed by photo pages
