You can view a PDF online without downloading it right now, using only your browser. No extra software needed. If that's not working the way you expect, a quick settings change will fix it. And if you need to open a PDF that a website is forcing you to save, there's a workaround for that too.

## Why browsers sometimes download PDFs instead of opening them

Modern browsers include a built-in PDF viewer. [Chrome](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/7692821), Firefox, Edge, and Safari all have one. When you click a PDF link, your browser checks two things: whether its own PDF viewer is enabled, and whether the website is sending an instruction to force a download.

If your browser is set to download PDFs, a single settings change fixes it. If the website is forcing the download (some do this on purpose with an HTTP response header), you need a different approach.

## Method 1: Change the browser setting to open PDFs

This is the most common fix. Takes about 30 seconds.

**Google Chrome**

1. Click the three-dot menu in the top right and choose Settings.
2. Go to Privacy and security > Site settings.
3. Scroll to Additional content settings, then PDF documents.
4. Select "Open PDFs in Chrome."

**Mozilla Firefox**

1. Click the three-line menu > Settings.
2. Type "PDF" in the search bar at the top of Settings.
3. Under Portable Document Format (PDF), pick "Open in Firefox" from the dropdown.

**Microsoft Edge**

1. Click the three-dot menu > Settings.
2. Go to Cookies and site permissions > PDF documents.
3. Turn off "Always download PDF files."

**Safari on macOS**

Safari opens PDFs inline by default. If a link still downloads, the site is overriding that behavior. Use the Google Drive method below.

After updating the setting, click any PDF link and it should open in a new tab instead of landing in your Downloads folder.

## Method 2: Open the link in a new tab

Before changing any setting, try this first. Right-click the PDF link and choose "Open link in new tab." Some websites use a download button that wraps the URL, but the raw link still points to a viewable PDF. Your browser will display it directly when you open it this way.

This works more often than you'd expect, and it takes five seconds.

## Method 3: Use Google Drive as a viewer

Google Drive can display PDFs from your computer or from a URL. It's useful in two situations: when you have the file on your device and want to avoid local software, and when a site is forcing a download that you can't get around any other way.

**Opening a file you already have:**

1. Go to [drive.google.com](https://drive.google.com) and sign in (a free account works).
2. Drag the PDF onto the Drive page or click New > File upload.
3. Double-click the uploaded file. Drive opens it in a built-in viewer.

**Opening a PDF from a URL without downloading it:**

Construct a URL in this format:

```
https://drive.google.com/viewer?url=PASTE_THE_PDF_URL_HERE
```

Paste the full, direct URL to the PDF file in place of `PASTE_THE_PDF_URL_HERE`. Google's servers fetch the PDF and display it. Nothing saves to your computer.

One important note: this only works for publicly accessible PDFs. If the PDF is behind a login or a paywall, Google Drive's servers can't reach it either.

## Method 4: Use a free online PDF viewer

Several web-based viewers let you upload and read a PDF without installing anything. [Mozilla's PDF.js viewer](https://mozilla.github.io/pdf.js/) (the same engine Firefox uses internally) is open-source and runs entirely in the browser. Smallpdf and Adobe also offer online viewers that work without an account.

The practical difference between these and the Google Drive approach comes down to privacy: most online viewers process your file on their servers, while your browser's built-in viewer and the Drive approach keep the file either local or in an account you already control. For sensitive documents like medical records or legal contracts, the browser's built-in viewer is the safer choice.

## What to do when the PDF is too large to load quickly

A large PDF can time out or hang in a browser tab even when your settings are right. Two fixes work well depending on what's in the file:

**Split it.** If you need just a few pages from a 200-page report, use the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) to pull those pages into their own file. A 10-page excerpt loads instantly. You can download that excerpt and open it in a tab, or share it with someone else without them having to wait through the full document.

**Compress it.** If the PDF is full of photos, scanned receipts, or presentation slides with background images, the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) can reduce the file size significantly. One thing worth knowing: the compressor works by converting pages to JPEG images. That helps a lot for photo-heavy files, but it makes text-only documents larger. Use compression for scanned and image-heavy PDFs; for text documents, splitting is the better approach.

## How to share a PDF so others can view it in-browser

When you share a PDF, the person receiving it can run into the same download-vs-view problem. Google Drive solves this cleanly.

1. Upload the PDF to Google Drive.
2. Right-click the file and choose Share.
3. Change the permission to "Anyone with the link" and set the role to Viewer.
4. Copy the link and send it.

Anyone with that link can open and read the PDF in their browser without a Google account. They see the Drive viewer, they can't edit the file, and there's no forced download.

[OneDrive](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/share-files-in-onedrive-personal-3822cc39-2aac-4793-80e5-2dc6d96cce7f) and [Dropbox](https://help.dropbox.com/share/create-and-share-links) offer the same idea with slightly different interfaces. All three let you generate a view-only link that opens in a browser.

## Converting PDF pages to images

If you need to share individual pages in a format that anyone can view without a PDF viewer at all, the [PDF to JPG tool](/pdf-to-jpg) converts each page to a JPEG image. Images open in any browser, photo app, or messaging app with no settings to change. This works well for sharing a specific chart, a contract page for review, or anything where the other person only needs one page rather than the full document.
