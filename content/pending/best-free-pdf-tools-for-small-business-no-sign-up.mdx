The best free PDF tools for small business let you sign contracts, merge documents, protect files, and compress scans without a subscription or an account. Every tool here runs in your browser. Your files stay on your device.

Running a small business means PDFs land in your inbox constantly. Contracts need signing. Invoices need attaching to emails. Scanned receipts need organizing. A paid Adobe Acrobat subscription runs $20+ per month for tasks most business owners do a few times a week. The tools below handle every common task for free, with nothing to install and no sign-up form.

## What PDF tasks do small businesses actually need?

The core list is shorter than you might think: sign incoming contracts, merge related files into one document, protect sensitive files with a password before sharing, fill out vendor or government forms, and occasionally compress a scan or convert phone photos into a PDF.

That covers the vast majority of PDF work in a small office. Each task below maps to a free browser tool.

## Signing contracts without printing

The [Sign PDF tool](/sign-pdf) lets you draw or type a signature and place it anywhere on the page. Drop in the contract, add your signature, download the signed file.

This is the right choice for routine business agreements: service contracts, vendor terms, subcontractor NDAs, lease renewals. For transactions where a verifiable audit trail matters (real estate closings, financial instruments, regulated industries), you'll want a full e-signature platform like [DocuSign](https://www.docusign.com/) or Adobe Sign, which provide timestamped records and identity verification.

For most small business paperwork, a drawn signature on a PDF is exactly what the other party expects, and this gets it done in under two minutes.

## Merging documents into one file

Clients and vendors often want everything in one PDF rather than four separate attachments. The [Merge PDF tool](/merge-pdf) combines files in the order you drag them. Add your proposal, your pricing sheet, and your terms document, and you're done.

One concern people have: will combining large files push the result over an email size limit? In testing, three files totaling 971.9 KB merged into a 970.5 KB output. The merge step itself adds essentially nothing to the file size. If the combined total is too large to send by email before you merge, it'll still be too large after, and the solution is to compress any image-heavy files first (see the section below).

One important caveat: if you're merging a filled-in PDF form, the form fields get stripped during the merge. The safe move is to [flatten the form first](/flatten-pdf), which bakes the field values into the page as regular visible text that can't be lost, then merge.

## Protecting sensitive documents

Any file with client data, pricing, financial details, or personal information should get a password before it goes out over email. The [Protect PDF tool](/protect-pdf) encrypts the file using [AES-256](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Encryption_Standard), the same cipher that banks use for data at rest.

Encryption adds almost no file size overhead, about 1 KB regardless of the document's original size. Protecting a file won't tip it over an email attachment limit. The recipient will need the password to open it, so share that separately (by phone or in a different message, not in the same email as the file).

If you ever need to remove the password later, the [Remove PDF Password tool](/unlock-pdf) strips it in seconds, as long as you have the password you set.

## Filling out PDF forms without printing

Vendor registration forms, government applications, W-9s, and client intake forms often arrive as PDFs with clickable fields. The [Fill PDF Form tool](/fill-form) detects those fields and lets you type directly into them.

After filling, run the completed form through the [Flatten PDF tool](/flatten-pdf) before emailing it. Flattening locks the values into the page so they can't shift or disappear if the recipient's PDF viewer handles interactive forms differently.

## Compressing scanned receipts and image files

If your PDF is a scan, a set of product photos, or any document where the content is photographs, the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) can cut the file size significantly. In testing, a 6-page image-heavy PDF dropped from 913.4 KB to 315.3 KB at Medium quality, a 65.5% reduction.

The tool works by rasterizing each page as a JPEG. That's effective for image-heavy content, and counterproductive for text-based documents. A 20-page text report grew from 23.5 KB to 1.4 MB after compression. Do not use the compress tool on invoices, contracts, proposals, or any document that's mostly text. Those files are already small.

If a text-based PDF is too large to send by email, the likely cause is an embedded high-resolution logo or unusual fonts embedded in the file. Splitting off unnecessary pages with the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) is often a more effective fix.

Also worth knowing: running compression twice on the same file makes it bigger, not smaller. In testing, compressing an already-compressed image deck at the same setting increased the size by 90%. If Medium quality isn't enough, start fresh from the original file at High compression rather than re-running on the output.

## Converting phone photos and screenshots to PDF

After photographing a signed document, a whiteboard, or a stack of receipts with your phone, you'll often end up with JPG files instead of a PDF. The [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf) turns one or more JPG or PNG images into a single PDF, one image per page, in the order you choose.

Drag in the images, reorder them if needed, and download the PDF. Useful for submitting scanned receipts to an accountant, attaching multiple product photos as one file, or sending a set of photos as a single email attachment.

## Splitting out specific pages

If a client sends a 50-page contract and you only need to sign the signature page, or if you need to share a specific section without exposing the rest, the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) extracts any page range into a separate file.

This also helps when a single document is too large as a whole but could be split into smaller sections and sent separately.
