The fastest way to compress a PDF for a shared drive upload is to drop it into the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) in your browser. No sign-up, no file sent to a third-party server. Everything runs locally on your device.

There is one thing to check first, though: this approach works very well for scanned documents and image-heavy PDFs, but it will make a text-only document dramatically larger. The section below will help you figure out which type of PDF you have before you do anything.

## Which PDFs shrink well, and which don't?

This is the question that determines everything else.

**Image-heavy PDFs** compress well. Think scanned pages, exported PowerPoint decks, brochures, or reports where most of each page is a chart or photograph. The tool converts every page to a JPEG image, which is exactly how scanned content is stored already. The result is a much smaller file.

Measured on a 6-page image deck: the file went from 913.4 KB to 315.3 KB at Medium quality, a 65.5% reduction. At High (smallest), the same deck came in at 175.6 KB, an 80.8% reduction. For a shared drive holding dozens of scanned reports, that adds up fast.

**Text-based PDFs** do not compress well with this tool. A 20-page text report that started at 23.5 KB grew to roughly 1.4 MB after compression, about 60 times larger. That happens because text stored as [vector glyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyph) is tiny; text stored as a JPEG photograph of those glyphs is not.

To figure out which type you have, open the PDF and try to highlight text with your cursor. If words select, it's a text PDF. If nothing selects, it's a scanned image.

## How to compress a PDF for shared drive upload

1. Open the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf). Your file stays in your browser.
2. Drag your PDF onto the page, or click the upload area to browse.
3. Choose a compression level. **Medium** is the right starting point for most scanned files. If the result is still too large, go back to the original and run it again at **High**. Don't run it twice on the same output: re-compressing a file at Medium nearly doubles its size rather than shrinking it further.
4. Download the result and upload it to your shared drive.

For most scanned PDFs under 50 MB, the whole process takes under a minute.

## How much storage can you actually save?

Real results for a 6-page image deck, measured in-browser:

| Level | Before | After | Reduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low (best quality) | 913.4 KB | 703.5 KB | 23.0% |
| Medium | 913.4 KB | 315.3 KB | 65.5% |
| High (smallest file) | 913.4 KB | 175.6 KB | 80.8% |

A shared drive with 100 scanned PDFs averaging 2 MB each takes up 200 MB. Compressing them at Medium could bring that to under 70 MB. That is significant when your [Google Workspace admin](https://support.google.com/a/users/answer/7338880) has set a storage cap on the shared drive.

## What if compression makes the file larger?

Your PDF is almost certainly text-based. Skip compression entirely. These alternatives work better:

**Delete unnecessary pages.** Many reports carry appendices, cover pages, or blank sheets that nobody needs. Use the [Delete Pages tool](/delete-pages) to cut them out. Fewer pages mean a smaller file, without any quality change.

**Split the file.** If a 60-page document would be more useful as three 20-page files, the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) lets you define page ranges and save each as a separate document. Smaller individual files are easier to manage and easier for your teammates to open on slow connections.

**Organize and trim visually.** The [Organize PDF tool](/organize-pdf) gives you a thumbnail view of every page. Click to remove pages you don't need, drag to reorder, then download. It's faster than typing page ranges when you need to make several cuts at once.

**Use a zip archive.** Text PDFs compress well with standard zip compression because zip uses lossless algorithms suited to text. On Windows, right-click the file and choose "Send to > Compressed folder." On Mac, right-click and choose "Compress." Google Drive accepts zip uploads.

## The 50 MB rule: why file size matters beyond storage

[Google Drive's indexing limit for PDFs is 50 MB](https://developers.google.com/workspace/drive/api/guides/limits). Files above that threshold upload without any error, but Drive won't index the text inside them. Your teammates can open the file directly, but searching for a phrase in Drive's search bar won't surface it.

Keeping scanned PDFs under 50 MB is worth doing for searchability alone, separate from any storage concerns. A report that nobody can find through search might as well not exist.

## If your shared drive is already full

When a Google Workspace Shared Drive is near its storage limit, everyone trying to upload hits an error. Here's a practical order of operations:

1. Identify the largest PDFs already in the drive. In Google Drive, switch to list view and sort by file size.
2. Download the largest image-heavy PDFs, compress them at Medium or High, re-upload, then delete the originals.
3. Look for duplicate or outdated files and delete them outright.
4. Archive PDFs nobody has opened in a year by moving them to a personal drive or cold storage.
5. If none of that frees enough space, ask your admin to increase the storage allocation for the drive.

Compression combined with deletion usually resolves a full shared drive without involving IT.

## A note on compressing twice

It's tempting to run a compressed file through the tool a second time hoping to squeeze out more. Don't. A file already compressed at Medium grew from 315.3 KB to 598.9 KB when compressed again at the same level. The tool re-renders what is already a JPEG and adds data rather than removing it.

If Medium wasn't enough, start over from the original file and pick High instead.
