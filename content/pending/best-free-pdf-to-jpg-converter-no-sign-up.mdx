## What to look for in a PDF to JPG converter

Not all converters are equal. These are the things that actually matter:

**No server upload.** When a tool runs in your browser, your PDF never travels to a third-party computer. For personal documents, tax records, contracts, or anything private, that's a meaningful difference. Upload-to-server tools are fine for public documents, but you're trusting a company you may know nothing about to delete your file afterward.

**No account required.** You should not have to hand over your email address to convert a single file. Good tools skip the sign-up step entirely.

**Multi-page support.** A converter that only handles single-page PDFs is nearly useless. Most real documents have several pages.

**Clear output.** The resulting JPG should be readable without squinting. A blurry or low-resolution image defeats the purpose.

## In-browser vs upload-to-server: what's the practical difference?

When you use an upload-to-server converter, your PDF travels to a remote machine, gets processed, and you get a link to the result. The company's privacy policy governs what happens next.

When you use an in-browser converter, the conversion happens in JavaScript running on your own computer. Your file never leaves your browser tab. Our [PDF to JPG converter](/pdf-to-jpg) works this way.

For most everyday files it doesn't matter much. For anything you'd hesitate to email to a stranger, an in-browser tool is the safer choice.

## Does converting a PDF to JPG reduce quality?

Yes, a little. [JPEG is a lossy format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPEG), meaning some image data is discarded during encoding to keep file sizes manageable. At the quality settings most converters use, the difference is hard to see at normal reading sizes. You'd notice it if you zoomed in very closely, but not at 100%.

What affects output sharpness most is the scale or DPI (dots per inch) the converter uses when rendering the page. [DPI determines how many pixels are rendered per inch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dots_per_inch) of the original page:

- 72 DPI gives a small, screen-resolution image, good for quick previews.
- 150 DPI is a reasonable middle ground for sharing by email or posting online.
- 300 DPI is print quality, suitable when someone will actually print the image.

One thing worth knowing: pages that are mostly text look noticeably softer as JPGs than they do in the original PDF. PDF stores text as vector shapes, which stay crisp at any size. JPG stores pixels, which can look slightly fuzzy, especially at smaller sizes. If sharp text is the priority, share the PDF directly.

## Page conversion vs extracting embedded images

These two operations sound similar but they produce very different results.

**Page conversion** (what PDF to JPG converters do) renders the whole page as a flat picture. Text, charts, logos, and photos all get merged into a single image. You get one JPG per page, and the output looks like a screenshot of the document.

**Image extraction** pulls the image files that are embedded inside the PDF. A scanned document, for example, might have one JPEG per page embedded at whatever resolution the scanner used. Extracting those gives you the originals at full resolution. Pages that are just text produce nothing, because there's no embedded image to pull out.

Use extraction when you want the original photos from a PDF at the highest possible quality. Use page conversion when you want a visual record of each page as it appears. Our [Extract Images tool](/extract-images) handles extraction; the [PDF to JPG tool](/pdf-to-jpg) handles page conversion.

## When is a JPG more useful than the original PDF?

- **Social media and messaging apps.** Most platforms let you share images directly. PDFs are often harder to open on a phone.
- **Presentations.** Dropping a chart or a diagram from a PDF into Google Slides or PowerPoint is much easier when you have it as a JPG.
- **Email previews.** A JPG thumbnail gives recipients a quick look at the content without opening a separate app.
- **Image editing.** Canva, Photoshop, and similar tools open JPGs natively. They don't open PDF pages directly.
- **Legacy systems.** Some online portals or forms only accept image file types.

## JPG vs PNG: which format should you choose?

[PNG is a lossless format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PNG), so every pixel is preserved exactly. That makes it great for diagrams, screenshots, and anything with sharp text or lines. The downside is file size: a PNG of a typical PDF page is usually several times larger than a JPG of the same page.

If you're sharing online, attaching to an email, or posting to social media, JPG is almost always the better pick. If you need the image to look perfect when printed at large sizes, or if you're going to edit it further, PNG is worth the extra file size.
