If the IRS Document Upload Tool is rejecting your file, the limit is 15 MB per PDF (and 120 pages per file). The fastest free fixes are to split the PDF into smaller parts or, if your document is a scan, to compress it. Here's how to tell which one to use.

## What are the IRS file size limits?

The [IRS Document Upload Tool](https://www.irs.gov/help/irs-document-upload-tool) lets you submit JPEG, PNG, or PDF files. Key limits:

- **15 MB per file**
- **120 pages per PDF**
- **Up to 40 files** per submission

If you're a tax professional or business filing via the Modernized e-File (MeF) system, the limits are higher: 60 MB per individual PDF attachment, with a 1 GB cap across all attachments in a single return. Most people hit the 15 MB wall from the Document Upload Tool, not MeF.

Some third-party tax software (TurboTax, H&R Block, TaxAct) has its own PDF attachment limits separate from the IRS limits. Check your software's help page if the rejection comes from the software rather than from irs.gov itself.

## Two fixes, and how to pick the right one

You have two tools: compress and split. The wrong choice can make your problem worse.

**Compress works when your PDF is mostly images** (a scan of paper receipts, a photograph of a document, a multi-page scan from a phone camera). These files are large because image data takes up space, and compression shrinks images efficiently.

**Compress does NOT work for text-based PDFs** (printed tax forms, a Form 1040 you filled out digitally, a Word document saved as PDF, or any form generated by tax software). Browser-based compression works by rasterizing each page to a JPEG image. A text form that is 23.5 KB before compression becomes 1.4 MB after, because text stored as vector data is tiny, while a photograph of that same text is not. Running compression on a text form will get it rejected for being even larger.

**Split works for any PDF**, text or scanned. It breaks the file at page boundaries with no quality change. The IRS accepts multiple uploads, so you can split a 25-page document into two 13-page files and upload them both.

When in doubt, split.

## How to compress a scanned PDF for IRS submission

If your PDF is a scan (phone photo, flatbed scanner, or similar), compression can cut file size significantly. Use the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf), which runs entirely in your browser. Your file is never sent to a server, which matters when you're dealing with tax documents.

1. Open the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf).
2. Drop your scanned PDF onto the page.
3. Choose **Medium** compression. (High produces a smaller file but more visible quality loss; for documents the IRS reads, Medium is the better tradeoff.)
4. Download the result and check the file size before uploading to the IRS.

One pass is all you need. Running compression a second time on an already-compressed file makes it larger, not smaller.

If the compressed file is still over 15 MB, switch to the split approach below.

## How to split a PDF for IRS submission

Splitting keeps quality exactly as-is and works on any kind of PDF. The [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) lets you break a document at any page you choose.

1. Open the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf).
2. Drop your PDF onto the page.
3. Choose where to split. For a 30-page document that needs to come in under 15 MB, split at page 15 to get two roughly equal files.
4. Download both files.
5. In the IRS Document Upload Tool, upload each file separately. The tool allows up to 40 files, so splitting into a few parts is fine.

If you're not sure how many pages to put in each part, estimate: a scanned page is usually 200 to 500 KB, so a 15 MB limit fits roughly 30 to 75 scanned pages. A page of text is far smaller, so a text PDF rarely needs splitting for size unless it has embedded images.

## Scanning tips to avoid the problem next time

If you're scanning paper documents to send to the IRS, these settings keep file sizes manageable:

- **Resolution:** 200 to 300 DPI is enough for legibility. Scanning at 600 DPI makes files 4 times larger than 300 DPI with no benefit for the IRS reader.
- **Color mode:** Use grayscale or black-and-white for text documents. Color scans are 2 to 3 times larger for documents that are mostly text.
- **Format:** Save as PDF, not TIFF. TIFF files are uncompressed and much larger than equivalent PDFs.

Most phone scanning apps (iOS Files, Google Drive, Adobe Scan) let you adjust resolution and color in settings. Setting them before you scan saves editing time later.

## What if the IRS rejects the upload even after fixing the file?

The Document Upload Tool validates file size, page count, and format. If you've addressed the size and it still fails:

- Check the page count. Files over 120 pages are rejected even if the size is under 15 MB. Split the document first.
- Check the PDF isn't encrypted or password-protected. Some PDF editors save with restrictions that the IRS portal can't read. Use the [Remove Password tool](/unlock-pdf) to strip it before uploading.
- Try a different browser. The IRS Document Upload Tool is a web app; an older browser version can cause upload failures unrelated to the file itself.

The IRS has also expanded secure digital correspondence options in recent years, meaning more document types can be submitted digitally rather than by mail. If the upload tool isn't working and you're on a deadline, the IRS's [secure messaging and callback options](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-expands-secure-digital-correspondence-for-taxpayers) are worth checking as a backup.

## Why not just use Adobe or a random online compressor?

You can, but tax documents contain personal information (Social Security numbers, income figures, addresses). Uploading to a cloud service you don't know means your data passes through their servers. Both the [Compress PDF](/compress-pdf) and [Split PDF](/split-pdf) tools on this site process files locally in your browser. Nothing is sent to any server. That's the right choice for IRS documents.
