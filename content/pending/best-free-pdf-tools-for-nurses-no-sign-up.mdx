The best free PDF tools for nurses let you fill forms, sign documents, merge patient paperwork, and compress scanned files, all in your browser with no sign-up and no files sent to a server. This page lists the tasks nurses run into most, and the specific free tools that handle each one cleanly.

[Nursing documentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nursing_documentation) spans consent forms, incident reports, shift handoff sheets, care plans, and clinical notes, most delivered as PDFs. Adobe Acrobat handles all of it, but its subscription costs over $20 a month. These browser-based alternatives cost nothing and require no account.

## Fill out a PDF form without Adobe

Many hospital forms are distributed as fillable PDFs: incident reports, consent templates, pain assessment tools. If a colleague sends you a PDF with blank fields and you try to open it in a browser, you can usually read it but not type into it without the right software.

The [Fill PDF Form tool](/fill-form) detects text boxes, checkboxes, and dropdown fields automatically. Open the file, type your values, and download the filled version. No account needed. Because the processing happens in your browser, the document stays on your device.

One thing to know before merging a filled form into a larger packet: this tool uses interactive form fields that some software strips during merge. Flatten the form first (more on that below) so the typed values are painted permanently into the page.

## Sign a consent form or other document

Drawn signatures, not just typed names, are often required on consent forms and patient authorization documents. The [Sign PDF tool](/sign-pdf) lets you draw your signature with a mouse or finger, then drag it to the right spot on the page.

This is useful for nurses acting as witness signatories on consent forms, or any time you need to add a hand-drawn signature to a document without printing, signing by hand, and scanning back in. The result is a standard PDF that opens in any reader.

## Merge patient documents into one PDF

Pulling together a patient transfer packet, a shift handoff report, or a portfolio of records often means combining files from different systems: a care plan from one place, a discharge summary from another, and a list of current medications from a third.

The [Merge PDF tool](/merge-pdf) combines any number of PDFs in the order you set. Drag files in, arrange them, and download one combined PDF. Measured output sizes show the merge adds essentially no overhead: a collection of files totalling 971.9 KB came out at 970.5 KB after combining.

**Important:** merging strips interactive form fields. If any of your files contain a filled PDF form, use the [Flatten PDF tool](/flatten-pdf) on that file before adding it to the merge. Flattening bakes the typed values into the page as static content, so nothing is lost when the files are combined. This matters most when a consent form or intake questionnaire is part of the packet.

## Compress scanned records before emailing or uploading

Hospital systems and insurance portals often cap file sizes, and scanned records can get large fast. A six-page scan of photos or printed lab results is the right candidate for compression.

The [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) works by rasterizing each page to a JPEG image, which shrinks image-heavy files significantly. A 913 KB image-heavy PDF came down to 315 KB at Medium quality, and to 176 KB at High.

The same tool does the opposite on text-only documents. A 20-page text report grew from 23.5 KB to 1.4 MB when compressed, because rasterizing text converts efficient vector glyphs into much larger image data. Use compression only on scanned image pages, not typed reports, contracts, or forms.

If a text PDF is too big to email, the problem is usually embedded fonts, a large logo, or revision history, not the text itself. Splitting it into smaller parts (below) is usually the right move.

## Convert phone photos to a PDF

Nurses photograph things at the bedside: wound measurements against a ruler, a patient's medication bottles, a printed lab result that hasn't made it into the EHR yet. Getting those photos into a PDF for documentation takes one step.

The [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf) accepts JPEG and PNG files. Drag in the photos, set the order if there are several, and download a single PDF. Each photo gets its own page. The tool works on both desktop and mobile browsers, so you can do this directly from a phone or tablet.

## Protect a PDF with a password

When sharing a document that contains any patient information outside a secured system, adding a password gives a basic layer of access control. The [Protect PDF tool](/protect-pdf) adds [AES-256 encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Encryption_Standard) and requires a password to open the file. The size overhead is small, about 1 KB regardless of file size, so it does not push files over email limits.

To remove a password, the [Unlock PDF tool](/unlock-pdf) accepts the known password and returns the file without it, within a few bytes of its original size.

Note that password protection and HIPAA compliance are not the same thing. [HIPAA](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/index.html) requires a full set of administrative, physical, and technical safeguards for protected health information. A password-protected PDF is a sensible precaution for incidental transfers, not a compliance program.

## Delete unnecessary pages before sharing

Long documents often contain pages that shouldn't go to a particular recipient: a summary report that includes an appendix only the author needs, or a template with instruction pages that clutter the final packet.

The [Delete Pages tool](/delete-pages) removes individual pages from a PDF. You pick the pages to cut, and download the trimmed result. If you need to pull out a few pages as a separate document rather than delete them, [Extract Pages](/extract-pages) does the reverse: you choose the pages to keep and save them as a new file.

## A word on privacy

These tools run in your browser. When you open a file, it loads into your device's memory and processing happens there, not on a remote server. If you're handling documents with patient names, dates of birth, or clinical data, that distinction matters. The files you work with don't transit an upload pipeline or sit in a cloud queue.

That said, browser tools are not a substitute for your organization's document management policies. For anything going into a permanent record, use the system your employer provides for that purpose.
