Batch converting images to PDF means running dozens of files through a single tool and getting one finished document out. Whether you're packaging scanned receipts, assembling a photo portfolio, or preparing a set of pages from your phone camera, the [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf) handles all of them at once, free, without an account, and without sending your files anywhere.

## What does "batch convert" actually mean?

The phrase covers two different tasks, and it's worth knowing which one you need before you start.

**All images into one PDF.** This is the most common case. You have 10, 20, or 50 images and you want them combined into a single document, one image per page, in a specific order. A scanned multi-page form, a photo report, a portfolio, a set of product shots: all of these go into one PDF. This is what the Images to PDF tool does.

**Each image into its own PDF.** Sometimes you need every photo to produce its own separate PDF file. This is less common and usually needed for archiving individual items or meeting a system that expects one file per record. For this task you'd convert the images one at a time, or use desktop software that writes a batch of single-page files.

Most people searching for "batch convert images to PDF" want the first option, a single document with many pages, so that's what the rest of this guide focuses on.

## How to batch convert images to one PDF

This takes about 30 seconds for a folder of images.

1. Open the [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf) in your browser.
2. Drop all your image files into the upload area at once. You can select an entire folder in the file picker, or drag a selection from your desktop.
3. Wait for the thumbnails to appear. The tool shows a small preview of each image so you can confirm everything uploaded correctly.
4. Drag the thumbnails to set the order you want the pages in. The first thumbnail in the list becomes page 1.
5. Click the convert button and download your PDF.

The files are processed inside your browser. Nothing is sent to a server, so the images stay on your device throughout.

## Which image formats are supported?

The tool accepts JPG (also written JPEG) and PNG files. You can drop in a mix of both, and they'll all end up in the same PDF.

If you have HEIC files from an iPhone or TIFF files from a scanner, you'll need to convert those to JPG or PNG first. On an iPhone, you can change the camera format in Settings so it shoots in JPG instead of HEIC. For TIFF, most image editors (including Windows Photos and macOS Preview) can export a TIFF as a JPG.

## How do I convert a whole folder of images?

The upload dialog lets you select multiple files at once. On Windows, open File Explorer, go to the folder, select all the files you want (Ctrl+A for all, or Ctrl+click for a selection), and drag them into the tool. On a Mac, do the same from Finder. On a phone, you can tap and hold to select multiple photos from your gallery.

If your folder has more images than you want in the final PDF, just select the specific files you need rather than selecting all.

## Does the order matter?

Yes, and it's worth getting right before you click convert. The tool will use whatever order you set in the thumbnail panel. By default, files usually appear in the order they were uploaded, which often follows alphabetical or date order from your file system.

For scanned documents, pages often come off the scanner numbered sequentially (scan001.jpg, scan002.jpg, and so on), so alphabetical order puts them in the right sequence automatically.

For photos you've taken yourself, check the sort order and drag thumbnails to fix anything that's out of sequence. Rearranging before converting is much faster than editing the PDF afterward.

## Will image quality change in the PDF?

The tool embeds each image as its own page. It doesn't re-encode or re-compress the image content, so quality is preserved. The PDF file will be roughly the same total size as the images you put in, which is typical for image-based PDFs.

This is worth knowing if you're planning to print the result. Images you captured at high resolution will print well. Images from a phone camera at standard settings (around 12 megapixels) produce sharp prints at letter size or A4. If your images are low resolution, the PDF won't fix that, but it won't make them worse either.

## When would I convert images to PDF rather than keeping them as images?

A few common reasons:

**Sharing.** A single PDF is easier to send and harder to accidentally shuffle than a folder of 20 loose image files. Recipients don't need to open them in a specific order.

**Printing.** Print dialogs handle PDFs reliably. Printing a folder of images one by one and maintaining page order is tedious. A PDF with all the pages in order makes the whole job a single print command.

**Submitting documents.** Courts, employers, insurance companies, and schools often require PDF format, even for documents that originated as photographs. [Converting scanned images to PDF](/jpg-to-pdf/scanned-pages) is standard for submissions.

**Archiving.** A PDF is a stable, widely-readable container. The [ISO 32000 standard](https://www.iso.org/standard/75839.html) for PDF is maintained by an independent standards body, meaning the format will remain readable long after proprietary image formats may become less common.

## What if I already have the images as individual PDFs?

If each image is already its own single-page PDF, you can combine them without going back to images first. Drop the individual PDF files into the [Merge PDF tool](/merge-pdf), arrange them in the order you want, and download the combined document. The result is the same as converting and combining in one step, and you avoid any quality changes from a second conversion.

## Does it work on mobile?

Yes. The tool runs in any modern browser, including Safari on iPhone and Chrome on Android. You can pull images from your phone's photo library through the file picker, or long-press a photo in your gallery and share it to your browser.

The main practical limit on mobile is screen space for rearranging thumbnails. For a handful of images, dragging to reorder is straightforward. For 30 or more images where sequence matters, a desktop browser is easier to work with.

## What about large batches?

The tool runs in-browser, so it's limited by the memory available in your browser tab rather than a server-side file size cap. In practice, a few dozen typical JPG photos (2 to 5 MB each) convert without issue on most devices. Very large batches with hundreds of high-resolution images may slow down or require more memory than a phone tab can provide. For those, splitting into a few smaller groups and using the [Merge PDF tool](/merge-pdf) to join the results is a reliable workaround.

- Files are all JPG or PNG (convert HEIC/TIFF to JPG first if needed)
- You've selected all the files you want in the final PDF
- Thumbnails are in the right order
- Click convert and download

The whole process takes under a minute for most batches, no account required, and your files stay local the whole time.
