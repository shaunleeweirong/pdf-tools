You can combine multiple JPG images into one PDF for free without downloading any software or creating an account. Upload your photos or scans to the [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf), put them in the right order, and download a single file. Each image becomes a page.

This comes up more often than you'd think. You photographed a multi-page contract with your phone. You scanned a form page by page. You took pictures of a receipt to submit for reimbursement. Instead of attaching a dozen separate files, you want one tidy document.

## How to combine multiple JPG images into one PDF

The [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf) takes three steps:

1. Open the tool and click the upload area, or drag your images straight onto it. It accepts JPG and PNG files, and you can drop as many as you like in one go.
2. Drag the thumbnails to rearrange them. The order on screen matches the page order in the finished PDF.
3. Click Convert. The PDF downloads to your device.

Nothing leaves your browser. The conversion runs locally with JavaScript, so your images never touch a server.

## Why put images in a PDF at all?

Sending ten separate JPEGs is awkward for the person on the other end: they have to open each file, figure out the order, and keep track of which ones they've reviewed. A single [PDF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF) fixes all of that. The pages stay in sequence, the document prints correctly, and most email clients and portals accept it natively.

PDF files also store exact page dimensions. A JPEG can look different depending on which app opens it and how it's zoomed. A PDF reader renders pages at the right size every time, and recipients can scroll through pages in a sidebar, bookmark a section, or print everything in one go.

If you need to submit documents to a government agency, a bank, a university, or an employer, chances are they ask for PDF. Sending a folder of phone photos is rarely accepted.

## What about PNG images?

The tool takes PNG files too. You can mix JPG and PNG uploads in the same batch. Each image, regardless of format, becomes one page. This is handy when you have a mix of phone photos (almost always JPEG) and screenshots or scanned documents (often PNG). Upload them together and arrange the thumbnails.

## On Windows without extra software

Windows 10 and 11 include a [Microsoft Print to PDF printer](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/pdf-printer-in-windows-a22a5eb1-0832-4e31-b24c-8e0a03b9e37b) that can turn any printable file into a PDF. You can select multiple image files in File Explorer, right-click, choose Print, and pick "Microsoft Print to PDF" as the printer. Windows Photo Viewer will open and start printing.

The limitation is that Windows Photo Viewer usually sends each image as its own print job, so you often end up with multiple PDFs rather than one. You'd then need to merge them. If you want everything combined in a single step, the browser tool is faster and simpler.

## On Mac with Preview

Mac's built-in Preview app can do this without any additional software. Open all your images in Preview at once (select them all in Finder, then right-click and choose Open With > Preview). Switch to thumbnail view with View > Thumbnails. All the images should now appear as pages in the sidebar.

If they opened as separate Preview windows instead, drag all the thumbnail images from the other windows into one single Preview window, putting them in the order you want. Then go to File > Export as PDF (or File > Print > Save as PDF).

[Apple's Preview support page](https://support.apple.com/guide/preview/combine-pdfs-prvw43696/mac) walks through the steps. It works fine for a small batch but gets fiddly once you have more than five or six images, since you're moving thumbnails around by hand.

## On iPhone or Android

On mobile, the [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf) works in a mobile browser. Tap the upload button, select multiple photos from your camera roll (on iOS, tap and hold the first photo, then tap the rest to add them), and the conversion runs in the tab.

On iOS 16 and later, there's also a built-in method in the Files app. Select the images, tap the three-dot menu, and look for the Create PDF option. It produces a quick PDF without any third-party tool. The order depends on what order you selected them, so it's worth checking the result before you send it.

Android handles this differently depending on the manufacturer and Android version. A browser-based tool is the most consistent option across devices.

## Will the images look different in the PDF?

Each image is placed on its own page. The display quality in the PDF depends on the resolution of the source file. If your photos look sharp on your phone screen, they'll look sharp in the PDF. If a scan came out blurry, the PDF will reflect that.

One thing to know: the [PDF format](https://www.iso.org/standard/75839.html) stores images as embedded objects. How crisp text and fine detail appear when printed depends on the dots-per-inch (DPI) of the source image. Phone cameras typically produce images at 72 DPI as displayed on screen, but the actual pixel dimensions are high enough that printing looks fine at standard page sizes.

## Does the file size get much bigger?

Roughly, the combined PDF file will be close to the total size of your source images. No images are removed or combined onto fewer pages, so the size goes up with each image you add.

If the resulting PDF is too large to attach to an email, most providers cap attachments at 25 MB per message. Try sending images in smaller batches, or run the finished PDF through a compression step. Keep in mind: the [Compress PDF](/compress-pdf) tool works best on PDFs that contain photos and scans. It rasterizes pages to JPEG, which shrinks image content but makes text-only documents significantly larger. Since your source images are photos or scans, compression is likely to help here if you need to reduce file size.
