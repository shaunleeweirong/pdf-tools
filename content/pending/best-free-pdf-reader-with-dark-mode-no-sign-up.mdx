The best free PDF readers with dark mode and no sign-up are SumatraPDF (Windows), Okular (Linux and Windows), Firefox's built-in viewer, and the Shadow Reader web app. Each takes a slightly different approach to dark mode, and picking the right one depends on your device and how much you want to install.

Before you start, it helps to know the difference between two types of "dark mode" you'll find in PDF readers:

- **True dark theme**: the app applies its own dark background and light text. Images stay correct. Text stays sharp.
- **Color inversion**: the reader inverts every color on the page. White becomes black, black becomes white, and photograph colors flip to look like a negative. It works in a pinch but is hard on images and anything that uses color coding.

True dark themes are almost always the better choice. The list below notes which approach each app uses.

## Which PDF readers have dark mode built in?

| Reader | Platform | Dark mode type | Sign-up needed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| SumatraPDF | Windows | True dark theme | No |
| Okular | Linux, Windows | Night mode / custom theme | No |
| Firefox viewer | All platforms | System color override | No |
| Shadow Reader | Browser (any) | Overlay filter | No |
| Adobe Acrobat Reader | Windows, Mac | True dark theme | Yes (Adobe ID) |

For a no-account, no-hassle setup, SumatraPDF, Okular, Firefox, and Shadow Reader are the four to know. Adobe Acrobat Reader has a polished dark mode, but it requires an Adobe account to use the app past the initial install. Foxit Reader lets you skip account creation, but it prompts you to sign in on almost every startup.

## How to turn on dark mode in each reader

**SumatraPDF (Windows)**

1. Open SumatraPDF and click Settings > Advanced Settings. This opens a plain-text settings file.
2. Find the `TextColor` and `BackgroundColor` lines.
3. Set `TextColor = #f0f0f0` and `BackgroundColor = #1e1e1e` (or any dark hex value you prefer).
4. Save and close the file. SumatraPDF applies the new colors immediately.

[SumatraPDF](https://www.sumatrapdfreader.org/) is free, open source under the GPLv3, and under 10 MB. It opens PDF, EPUB, MOBI, and comic-book formats.

**Okular (Linux, and Windows via the KDE package)**

1. Open Okular and go to Settings > Configure Backends > PDF.
2. Alternatively, use View > Color Mode, then pick "Change Paper Color" to set a custom dark background.
3. On Linux with KDE, Okular follows the system-wide dark theme automatically without any manual setting.

[Okular](https://okular.kde.org/) is free and open source, maintained by the KDE project.

**Firefox built-in PDF viewer**

1. Set your operating system to dark mode: System Preferences on macOS, Display Settings on Windows 11.
2. Open Firefox and go to Settings > General (scroll down to "Language and Appearance").
3. Under "Colors," choose "Use system colors." Firefox will pass the dark theme through to its PDF viewer.

This is the easiest path if you already use Firefox. Nothing extra to install, no extension needed.

**Shadow Reader (browser-based, no install)**

1. Go to [shadowreader.io](https://www.shadowreader.io/).
2. Upload or drag in your PDF.
3. The reader displays the document with a dark overlay. No account required.

Shadow Reader uses an overlay filter rather than a true dark theme, so image colors shift. For text-heavy documents it works well. For image-heavy PDFs, SumatraPDF or Okular give better results.

## Does dark mode actually reduce eye strain?

The honest answer is: somewhat, in specific conditions. Reading a bright white page in a dark room creates high contrast between the screen and the surrounding environment, and many people find that tiring over time. A darker background lowers the screen's overall luminance and reduces that glare.

That said, [according to the American Academy of Ophthalmology](https://www.aao.org/eye-health/tips-prevention/digital-eye-strain-facts), digital eye strain comes mainly from reduced blinking and prolonged close-up focus, not from screen color. The most effective habit is looking away roughly every 20 minutes at something about 20 feet away for 20 seconds. That does more for tired eyes than any color scheme.

One practical note: some people find white text on a pure black background creates a "halation" effect, where letters appear to bleed at the edges on bright displays. If pure dark mode makes text feel harder to focus on, try a dark gray background (like `#1e1e1e` or `#2b2b2b`) instead of `#000000`.

## What about mobile?

On **iPhone and iPad**, the built-in Files app opens PDFs in Quick Look, which follows iOS dark mode automatically. Go to Settings > Display and Brightness and pick Dark. No extra app needed. For more control, Apple Books (free, pre-installed) also respects dark mode and lets you change brightness independently of the rest of the OS.

On **Android**, opening a PDF in Google Drive or the Files by Google app gives you a PDF viewer that has its own dark mode toggle in the overflow menu (three dots). Samsung devices running One UI follow the system dark theme in Samsung's My Files app as well.

## A note on large PDFs that load slowly

Dark mode makes reading more comfortable, but it doesn't help if the PDF itself is slow to open. A common cause is embedded high-resolution images, which bulk out the file. The [Compress PDF](/compress-pdf) tool runs entirely in your browser and can cut the size of image-heavy documents significantly, without any account. For a document that's mostly scans or photos, that's worth trying before opening it in a reader.

Once you've finished reading, the [Edit PDF](/edit-pdf) tool lets you add text, draw, or annotate directly in your browser, no sign-up required.
