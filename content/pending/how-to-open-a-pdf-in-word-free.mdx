Opening a PDF in Word is free if you have Microsoft Word 2013 or later. Word converts the PDF into an editable document the moment you open it, no third-party software required. Here is exactly how to do it, what to expect from the result, and when you are better off not converting at all.

## How to Open a PDF in Word on Windows

The steps are the same in Word 2013, 2016, 2019, 2021, and Microsoft 365:

1. Open Microsoft Word.
2. Click **File**, then **Open**.
3. Click **Browse** to open the file picker.
4. Go to the folder with your PDF, select it, and click **Open**.
5. Word shows a dialog: "Word will now convert your PDF to an editable Word document." Click **OK**.

Word opens the converted document. Save it with **File > Save As** if you want to keep the Word version alongside your original PDF.

**Shortcut:** Right-click the PDF in File Explorer, choose **Open with**, and select **Word**. This skips the menu and does the same conversion in one step.

## How to Open a PDF in Word on Mac

The process on macOS mirrors Windows closely:

1. Open Microsoft Word.
2. Click **File** in the menu bar and choose **Open**.
3. Find your PDF in the dialog, select it, and click **Open**.

Word for Mac applies the same conversion as the Windows version. You can also drag a PDF onto the Word icon in your Dock, but File > Open is more predictable for larger files.

## What If I Have an Older Version of Word?

Word 2010 and earlier cannot open PDFs directly. If you are on an older version, you have two options: upgrade to a newer release of Word (Microsoft 365 has a free web version at office.com), or convert the PDF with an online tool first and then open the resulting `.docx` file in Word.

## Why Does the PDF Look Different After Opening in Word?

This is the most common frustration, and the cause is structural. [PDF is a fixed-layout format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF) that records exact positions of characters on a page. It has no concept of paragraphs, columns, or table cells in the way a word processor understands them.

When Word opens a PDF, it reverse-engineers the document: it looks at which characters are close together and guesses that they form a paragraph, or that horizontal lines must be a table. For simple documents, that guess is usually accurate. For complex ones, it often fails.

**When the result tends to be accurate:**
- Text-heavy documents with little layout (letters, contracts, plain reports)
- Single-column documents
- PDFs originally created from Word or similar software

**When conversion often breaks things:**
- Multi-column layouts such as newsletters or academic papers
- Tables with merged cells or complex borders
- Documents with many images placed precisely on the page
- Scanned PDFs (covered below)

[Microsoft's own documentation](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/opening-pdfs-in-word-1d1d2acc-afa0-46ef-891d-b76bcd83d9c8) notes this plainly: "The results look best with documents that are mostly text."

If your converted document looks wrong, try selecting all the text (Ctrl+A) and resetting the paragraph formatting. That fixes many line-break and spacing problems in a single step.

## How to Open a Scanned PDF in Word

Scanned PDFs are images of pages, not actual text. When you open one in Word, Word applies [OCR (optical character recognition)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_character_recognition) to extract the text automatically.

How well it works depends on the scan:
- Sharp scans at 300 dpi or higher usually convert accurately
- Low-resolution scans, photos taken at an angle, or pages with faded ink often produce garbled or missing text

If the OCR result is poor, check the original scan quality before blaming Word. A sharper scan of the same document typically produces a much better output.

## When Not to Convert: Editing the PDF Directly

Converting to Word and back is not always the right tool for the job. Every round-trip introduces the risk of reformatting. If you only need to fix a typo, update a date, add a line of text, or place a signature, converting to Word is more work than it needs to be.

## Inserting a PDF into Word Without Converting It

There is a second way to bring a PDF into Word: embedding it as an object. This is not a conversion. It inserts the PDF as a static image inside a Word document.

1. In Word, go to **Insert > Object > Object** (Windows) or **Insert > Object** (Mac).
2. Select **Create from File**.
3. Browse to your PDF and click **OK**.

The PDF appears as an embedded page. You can resize it and move it around, but you cannot edit the text inside it. This approach is useful when you want to attach a PDF to a Word report without converting it, such as appending a scan to a cover letter.

## Saving Back to PDF After Editing in Word

Once you have finished editing in Word and want a PDF again, use **File > Save As** and choose **PDF** from the format dropdown. This preserves fonts, layout, and structure more reliably than printing to PDF through the operating system's print dialog.

If the resulting PDF is larger than you expected, it may contain embedded fonts or high-resolution images. Our [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) reduces file size for image-heavy PDFs. Keep in mind that compressing a text-only PDF will make it larger, not smaller, since the tool rasterizes pages to JPEG. Use compression only when your document contains photos or images.

## A Quick Summary

Opening a PDF in Word is built into Word 2013 and later. Use File > Open, select your PDF, and Word converts it for free. Simple text documents convert well. Complex layouts often need manual cleanup afterward. If you only need small edits, skipping the conversion and editing the PDF directly is faster and safer for the layout.
