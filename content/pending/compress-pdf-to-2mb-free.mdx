## Why so many portals have a 2MB upload limit

Visa applications, embassy portals, job application systems, and university admissions sites all commonly cap uploaded documents at 2MB. The short answer is legacy infrastructure. Many of these platforms were built when storage was expensive and servers were slower. The limit was practical then and never updated as hardware improved.

A [government portal processing thousands of daily applications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_portal) needs every incoming file to be small enough that storage doesn't spike and document queues stay manageable. A 2MB cap is common on:

- Visa and passport document uploads (embassy portals, USCIS)
- Job application tracking systems
- University and professional exam registration sites
- Bank KYC document submissions
- Insurance claim portals

You'll typically see the error when you try to upload and the system either rejects the file silently or shows a message like "file must not exceed 2MB." The fix is compression, but only if you have the right kind of PDF.

## Check first: is your PDF a scan or a text document?

This step takes two seconds and will save you time.

Click on a line of text in your PDF.

**If the text highlights**, the file stores content as searchable font data. That makes text PDFs efficient: a 20-page typed report typically runs around 20 to 30 KB. If you compress a document like this, the tool [rasterizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rasterisation) every page into a JPEG image, and a photo of text is far larger than the text itself. In our own measurements, a 23.5 KB text report came out at 1,400.8 KB after compression. Don't compress it.

**If nothing highlights**, you have a scanned or image-based PDF. Compression is exactly what you need.

Scanned IDs, passport photo pages, handwritten forms, and documents you photographed rather than typed will all compress well.

## Do not run compression twice

It's tempting to run the result through compression again to squeeze out a bit more. This approach backfires.

We tested it. After one Medium pass, our 913.4 KB image PDF was 315.3 KB. Running it through again at Medium produced 598.9 KB, almost double. At High the second time, it reached 297.8 KB, saving just 5.6%.

The reason: the already-rasterized JPEG page gets re-rendered and re-encoded. Re-encoding a JPEG that's already been compressed adds noise rather than removing data.

The right approach is to go back to the original file and compress once at a stronger level. If Medium isn't enough, start fresh with High.

## When compression alone won't get you to 2MB

### Extract only the pages you actually need

Most portals ask for one specific document. A visa application might want a single bank statement page. A job portal might need your certificate, not the entire degree booklet. If your PDF has twelve pages and the portal needs three, use the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) to pull out those pages first. Then compress the smaller file.

Cutting a file from twelve pages to three reduces the starting size significantly before you apply any compression. This combination consistently works when a single compression pass leaves you just over the limit.

### Start from a lower-resolution source

If you're creating the PDF by scanning a physical document, the [DPI (dots per inch) setting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dots_per_inch) at scan time determines the starting size. Scanning at 150 DPI instead of 300 or 600 DPI produces a file several times smaller. A clean, well-lit scan at 150 DPI is fully legible. A 600 DPI scan of the same page will be five times larger without adding anything a reader can see.

If you're using your phone to photograph documents, check whether your scanner app has a quality or resolution setting. Dropping it to medium before you shoot is easier than trying to compress down from a huge original.

### Try submitting anyway

Some portals display a limit that predates their current infrastructure. If you're at 2.1MB after compression and trying again at High doesn't make sense, submit and see what happens. The error response will tell you quickly if the limit is enforced. You'll save time if it isn't.

## Tips for passport and ID document uploads

Passport photo pages and national ID cards have to stay legible for a human reviewer, so you can't compress hard without risking rejection.

The most reliable approach is a good source file:

1. Photograph or scan in bright, even light. Shadows on the document create large areas of uneven color that compress poorly.
2. Crop the image to the document itself. No desk, no background. A tight crop removes pixels that add no information.
3. Scan or photograph at 150 DPI if you have the option.
4. Run one pass at Medium. If the result is still over 2MB, try High.

Starting from a well-cropped 150 DPI source and compressing once at High usually gets a single-page ID or passport photo well under 2MB while staying readable. The goal for a clerk reviewing your document is clear text and a recognizable face. Reproducing every fiber in the paper background is not required.

## What if the file is already very large (over 10MB)?

Our compressor handles standard-sized documents well. For very large files, the combination of splitting and compressing is your best option. Extract the minimum pages the portal requires, then compress that smaller file.

If you have a multi-document package where each document belongs in a separate file anyway, split it up and submit the files separately if the portal allows. Portals that accept multiple attachments often have a per-file limit rather than a total limit, so a 3MB file split into two 1.5MB files may pass without any compression at all.

For reference: our measured compression of an image-heavy PDF at High level gave an 80.8% reduction, which means a 10MB file would land at roughly 1.9MB. Files that start at more than 10MB and need to reach 2MB may need splitting first.
