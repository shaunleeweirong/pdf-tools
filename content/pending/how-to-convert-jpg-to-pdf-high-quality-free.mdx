## Does converting JPG to PDF lower the quality?

It depends on what the converter does with your image data. A converter that takes your original JPEG and embeds it directly into a [PDF as an image object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF#Technical_overview) doesn't change a single pixel. Your JPG becomes the content of a PDF page, just wrapped in a different container.

The quality problems people run into come from converters that resample images during the process. Many tools shrink photos to 72 DPI (screen resolution) to reduce processing load or file size. If your photo started at 3024 x 4032 pixels, you won't notice the downsampling on a monitor, but printing it shows the blur immediately.

A simple test: convert a JPG, then open the resulting PDF at 400% zoom. If you see the same detail you see when zooming into the original image in a photo viewer, the converter preserved your data. If it looks softer than the original, the tool resampled your image on the way in.

## Why does my PDF look blurry after converting?

There are two distinct causes, and they have different fixes.

The first is a converter that resamples images. If the tool shrinks your photo during conversion, you lose detail that can't be recovered. The fix is switching to a converter that doesn't modify your image data.

The second is a source image that was already low-resolution. A 640 x 480 JPG looks acceptable in a small browser tab because screens display at 72 to 96 DPI. At print size, that same image might only have enough pixels for an inch or two of sharp content. Conversion adds no pixels, so the blur was already in the file.

To check: right-click your JPG, open Properties (Windows) or Get Info (Mac), and look at the pixel dimensions. Divide the pixel width by your intended print width in inches. If the result is below 200, expect visible blur when printed.

## What resolution does a JPG need for print quality?

[DPI (dots per inch)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dots_per_inch) describes how many pixels a printer maps to each inch of paper. The standard used by most professional print labs is 300 DPI at the final print size.

To hit 300 DPI at common paper sizes, your source JPG needs at least:

| Paper size | Minimum pixels needed |
|---|---|
| 4 x 6 inches | 1200 x 1800 px |
| Letter (8.5 x 11 in) | 2550 x 3300 px |
| A4 (210 x 297 mm) | 2480 x 3508 px |
| A3 (297 x 420 mm) | 3508 x 4961 px |

If you're sharing the PDF on screen, 72 to 96 DPI is fine. Modern displays show detail well at screen resolution, and a smaller file transfers faster.

For archiving or long-term storage, some organizations use [PDF/A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF/A), an ISO standard for archival PDFs that ensures documents remain readable over time. For most everyday purposes a standard PDF works fine.

## Does converting a JPG to PDF increase the file size?

Usually slightly. A PDF container adds a small header, page dimensions, and document metadata, so expect the PDF to be a few kilobytes larger than the source JPG. For a single photo, the difference is negligible. For a folder of images, the PDF total is close to the sum of the individual JPG sizes.

If you need a smaller PDF after converting, compress the source JPGs before you convert them, not after. Running the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) on a PDF that already contains JPEGs re-encodes those images, which can make the file larger rather than smaller. Better to start with smaller images at the source.

## When quality doesn't matter as much

Not every conversion is for print. If you're converting a screenshot to attach to an email, or turning a low-resolution logo into a PDF placeholder, screen resolution is more than enough and the output will look fine.

The high-quality path matters when you're preparing something for professional printing, a medical or legal document with scanned originals, or an archival copy of photographs. For those cases, check your source resolution first, use a converter that doesn't resample, and verify the result by zooming in before you send it.
