The fastest free way to share a large PDF is to upload it to Google Drive and paste the download link into your email. It works for any file size, needs only a free Google account, and the recipient can open or download the file without signing up for anything.

If the PDF is full of scans or photos and you'd rather send it as an attachment, compressing it first is worth trying. Read on for all four methods and how to pick the right one.

## Why does my PDF fail to attach to email?

Both Gmail and [Outlook](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/file-attachment-size-limits-for-microsoft-365-email-accounts-ffe9e56e-4de8-4338-a614-99bc1ebb4c11) cap messages at 25 MB. That sounds like plenty, but email encodes attachments using [Base64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base64), a format that adds roughly 33% to the file size in transit. The real ceiling for a file that arrives as a direct attachment is around 18 MB.

A PDF over that size will either bounce back with a delivery failure, or Gmail will quietly strip your attachment and replace it with a Google Drive link before sending. Either way, the recipient does not get the attached file you intended.

If your PDF is only a few megabytes over the limit, compression or splitting can fix it quickly. If it is 50 MB or more, a link-based method is the more reliable choice.

## Method 1: Compress the PDF before attaching (image PDFs only)

If your PDF is a scan, a photo report, or a presentation packed with images, our browser-based [Compress PDF](/compress-pdf) tool can cut the file size enough to bring it under the email limit. Nothing is uploaded to a server and no account is needed.

Measured results using a 6-page image deck (913.4 KB): at High compression, it shrank to 175.6 KB, a reduction of 80.8%. At Medium, it became 315.3 KB, down 65.5%.

One important caveat: this compressor works by rasterizing every page to JPEG. That approach helps a lot when pages are already photos or scans, but it does the opposite for text-based documents. A 20-page text report that started at 23.5 KB grew to 1.4 MB after compression at Medium. If your PDF is a contract, invoice, form, or any document with mostly text and vector graphics, skip compression entirely and use one of the methods below.

### How to compress a PDF for email

1. Open the [Compress PDF](/compress-pdf) tool.
2. Drop in your file. Choose Medium for a good balance of size and quality, or High to get the smallest possible file.
3. Download the compressed version and check the file size before attaching it.

## Method 2: Share via Google Drive link

This works for any PDF regardless of size.

1. Open [Google Drive](https://drive.google.com) and sign in. A standard Google account gives you 15 GB free.
2. Click **New**, choose **File upload**, and select your PDF.
3. Once it uploads, right-click the file and select **Share**.
4. Set access to "Anyone with the link can view" and click **Copy link**.
5. Paste the link into your email instead of attaching the file.

The recipient clicks the link and can view the PDF in their browser or download it. They do not need a Google account. The uploaded PDF does count against your 15 GB storage quota, but sharing the link costs the recipient nothing.

Dropbox and OneDrive offer the same approach under the same free-tier storage limits and work just as well if you already use one of them.

## Method 3: Use WeTransfer (no account required)

[WeTransfer](https://wetransfer.com) is built for exactly this problem. On the free tier you can send files up to 2 GB, the link is active for seven days, and neither you nor the recipient needs an account.

Go to wetransfer.com, upload your PDF, enter the recipient's email address, and click Send. WeTransfer emails them a download link directly. This is the most convenient method when the recipient does not use Google Drive or when the file is simply too large for Drive to feel practical.

## Method 4: Split the PDF into smaller parts

Sometimes the recipient needs an actual attached file, not a link. A corporate IT policy might block external links, or the recipient might be filing the email into a system that can only read attachments.

In that case, split the PDF into two or three chunks so each one comes in under the 18 MB threshold.

Our [Split PDF](/split-pdf) tool runs entirely in your browser. Upload the file, set the page ranges for each chunk, and download each part. Nothing is re-encoded, so there is no quality change at all. Send each part in a separate email and include part numbers in the subject line so the recipient knows to expect multiple messages.

## Which method to use

| Situation | Best option |
|---|---|
| PDF is image-heavy and around 20 to 40 MB | Compress, then attach |
| PDF is text-based or mostly text | Drive link or WeTransfer |
| Recipient needs an actual attachment | Split into parts |
| Fastest option for any file | Google Drive link |
| No Google account and file is under 2 GB | WeTransfer |

The compress-then-attach path gets the job done in about a minute for image-heavy files. For anything else, a Drive link is the path of least friction. Split only when the recipient genuinely needs the file as an attachment.
