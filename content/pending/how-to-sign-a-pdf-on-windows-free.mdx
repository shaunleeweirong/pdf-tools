Signing a PDF on Windows is free and doesn't require Adobe Acrobat. The fastest method is a browser-based tool: open it in Chrome, Edge, or Firefox, draw your signature with a mouse, and download the signed file. No software to install, no account to create.

Below are the two main free methods, and how to pick the right one.

## Method 1: Sign in your browser (no install, no account)

This is the best option for contracts, work documents, or anything where you want a proper electronic signature.

Open the [Sign PDF tool](/sign-pdf) in any browser on your Windows PC:

1. Click **Select PDF** or drag your file onto the upload area.
2. Click the signature prompt. A panel opens where you can draw your signature with a mouse or touchpad, type your name (it renders in a handwriting-style font), or upload a photo of your handwritten signature.
3. Place the signature on the first page of the document.
4. Click **Download** to save the signed PDF to your computer.

Your file is processed locally in your browser. It does not get sent to a server, which matters if you're signing something sensitive, like a contract, a lease, or a tax document.

The whole process takes under a minute on a standard Windows machine.

## Method 2: Use Microsoft Edge's Draw tool (built-in, no sign-up)

Windows 10 and 11 both ship with Microsoft Edge as the default PDF viewer, and Edge has a built-in **Draw** tool that lets you write or scribble on any page of a PDF.

1. Right-click your PDF file and choose **Open with > Microsoft Edge**.
2. In the toolbar at the top, click the **Draw** icon (it looks like a pen nib).
3. Adjust the pen color and thickness if you want, then draw your signature anywhere on the page using your mouse, a stylus, or a touchscreen.
4. Press **Ctrl+S** to save.

This works without any account or add-on. The catch: Edge's draw tool adds an annotation layer, not an electronic signature in the legal sense. There's no record of who signed, when, or whether the document changed afterward. It's fine for a quick sign-off on a permission form or a note to yourself, but for documents where authenticity can be questioned, the browser tool in Method 1 is a better fit.

## What types of signatures can you create?

Most free sign tools, including ours, offer three ways to add a signature:

- **Draw**: Use your mouse or touchpad to write your name freehand. Takes a few tries to look natural if you're not used to signing with a mouse.
- **Type**: Type your name and pick a script-style font. Fast and consistent, though it looks more like a typed name than a signature.
- **Upload image**: Take a photo of your handwritten signature on paper, crop it tight, and upload it as a PNG or JPG. This is the closest match to a physical signature and looks the most natural in the final document.

Any of these counts as an electronic signature for legal purposes. The format matters less than the intent: the signer chooses to apply the signature to signal agreement.

## Is an electronic signature legally binding?

For most documents, yes. In the United States, the [Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce (ESIGN) Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/106th-congress/senate-bill/761) has given electronic signatures the same legal effect as handwritten ones since 2000. The law covers most business and consumer contracts, including leases, employment agreements, and service contracts.

In the European Union, the [eIDAS regulation](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/eidas-regulation) establishes a similar framework. A simple electronic signature (drawing or typing your name) satisfies the requirements for everyday agreements.

A few categories remain exceptions in most jurisdictions: wills, certain property transfers, court orders, and documents requiring notarization typically still need a traditional wet-ink signature. When the stakes are high, verify requirements with a lawyer rather than assuming an e-signature suffices.

## What to do if the PDF has form fields

Some PDFs, like government forms, rental applications, or HR paperwork, have actual fillable fields built into the file. If you try to sign one of these without filling in the fields first, you may not be able to edit them afterward.

The right order is:

1. Fill in any form fields using the [Fill PDF Form tool](/fill-form).
2. Then sign the completed form.
3. Download the finished document.

If you later plan to merge the signed form with other PDFs, such as attaching it to a cover letter, flatten the form first using the [Flatten PDF tool](/flatten-pdf). Merging strips interactive fields, and flattening bakes the values into the page so they can't be lost.

## Quick checklist before you click Download

- You're signing the right version of the document (final draft, not a working copy).
- Pages are in the correct order. If not, use the [Organize PDF tool](/organize-pdf) first.
- If multiple people need to sign, each can use the tool independently on their own device.
- Rename the downloaded file to something descriptive, like `lease-signed-aug2026.pdf`, so you can find it later.
- Check whether the recipient needs the file below a size limit. For email, [Gmail and Outlook both cap attachments at 25 MB](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/6584), so a signed PDF of normal length won't be a problem.

Once you've downloaded the signed file, you're done. The process is the same on Windows 10 and Windows 11, and works in any browser including Edge, Chrome, and Firefox.
