The best free PDF tools for students are the ones that get out of the way. No account to create, no watermark on your work, no waiting for a server. The tools below cover the six tasks students run into every semester, and they all run directly in your browser.

## What PDF tasks do students actually need?

Most students need the same handful of things: combining separate files into one submission, shrinking a PDF that's too large for a portal, signing a form without printing it, turning phone photos into a PDF, and pulling specific pages out of a longer document.

None of these require [Adobe Acrobat](https://www.adobe.com/acrobat.html) or any paid software. They don't even require a download.

## Merging multiple files into one submission

Course submissions often have several parts: an essay, a reference list, a signed consent form, a cover sheet. Most portals want one PDF. The [Merge PDF tool](/merge-pdf) combines as many files as you need, in whatever order you drag them.

One thing worth knowing: the merged output is essentially the same size as the sum of its parts. Combining a 400 KB essay and a 570 KB appendix gives you roughly a 970 KB file. Merging doesn't inflate the total.

One thing to watch: if any of your PDFs contain fillable form fields, the merge process strips those fields out. If you've filled in a form and want the values to survive the merge, flatten the form first with the [Flatten PDF tool](/flatten-pdf) so the typed text is baked into the page.

## Compressing a PDF for Turnitin, Canvas, or Blackboard

This one has a catch that most guides skip over: compression only helps if your PDF is full of images or scanned pages. If it's a typed essay, compressing it will make it larger, not smaller.

Here's why. The [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) works by converting each page to a JPEG image. A page of text stored as vector characters is tiny. The same page stored as a photograph of characters is not. A 20-page text report that starts at 23.5 KB grows to around 1.4 MB after compression. That's the wrong direction entirely.

For scanned assignments or photo-heavy files, compression works well. An image-heavy PDF that started at 913.4 KB came down to 315.3 KB at the Medium setting, a reduction of about 65%.

So the rule is: use compression for scanned notes, photos, and drawings. For typed documents, a better move is to split off pages you don't need (see below) or check whether the portal accepts a ZIP instead.

[Turnitin's file size limit](https://help.turnitin.com/submitting-a-paper/the-similarity-report/file-types-and-size.htm) is typically 20 MB, though some institutions configure it higher. Canvas and Blackboard limits vary by institution, so check your assignment instructions.

## Signing forms without printing

A lot of student admin involves forms: housing contracts, internship agreements, volunteer waivers. Printing, signing, and scanning is slow and wastes paper.

The [Sign PDF tool](/sign-pdf) lets you draw or type a signature directly onto the form. For forms with typed fields (name, date, student ID), the [Fill Form tool](/fill-form) lets you type into those fields and download a completed copy.

Neither requires an account. Neither adds a watermark.

## Converting photos and scans to PDF

If your professor wants a scan and you took photos on your phone instead, the [JPG to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf) converts your images into a single PDF. Drop in as many images as you need, arrange them in the right order, and download the result.

This also works for hand-drawn diagrams, lab notebook pages, and anything else you've photographed rather than scanned. The [PDF file format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF) was designed to hold both raster images and vector text, so photos embed cleanly without a quality penalty beyond whatever JPEG compression was already in the photo.

## Splitting and extracting specific pages

Sometimes you only need a section of a longer document. If you've downloaded a 200-page textbook and your assignment covers chapters 3 and 4, the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) lets you pull out just those pages.

You can split by page range, split every page into its own file, or extract a handful of pages into a new document. No limit on how many times you use it.

## A note on privacy

These tools all run in your browser. Your file is never sent to a server and never stored anywhere. That matters if you're handling anything sensitive: an exam paper, a health form, a financial aid document.

Browser-based processing is also faster for small files, since there's no upload and download round trip. A merge or compress on a typical student assignment takes a few seconds.

## What if I need more than one tool?

Use them in whatever order the job requires. There's no session to manage and no account linking one tool to the next. Finish one task, come back for the next, and your files never touched a server either time.
