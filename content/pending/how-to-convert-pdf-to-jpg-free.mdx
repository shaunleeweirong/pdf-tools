## When does it make sense to convert a PDF to JPG?

PDFs are good for documents. Images are what most apps, platforms, and people actually need when they want to share or post a page.

**Sharing on social media.** Instagram, WhatsApp, LinkedIn, and most other platforms cannot display PDFs inline. A converted JPG shows up as an image in the feed rather than a file download nobody clicks.

**Embedding in a presentation.** Google Slides and PowerPoint accept JPG images natively. Dragging in a PDF is messier: you may need a plugin, and the result often looks different across devices. An image just drops in cleanly.

**Putting an image in the body of an email.** You can embed a JPG directly so the reader sees it without opening an attachment. A PDF always needs to be opened separately, and some email clients strip attachments entirely.

**Archiving scanned forms or receipts.** If a PDF is a scan of a paper document, each page is already a photograph. Keeping it as a JPG can make sense if you will never need to search or copy text from it.

**Sharing a single page from a longer report.** A chart or cover page as a standalone image is easier to paste into a Slack message or a Word document than the full PDF.

## What affects image quality after converting?

[JPG is a lossy format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPEG), which means it compresses the image data to keep file sizes manageable. That compression trades some fine detail for smaller files. The tradeoff is well-understood: photographic content holds up well at typical compression levels, while sharp text and thin lines can show slight softness around edges.

A few things shape how the output looks:

**The content of the original PDF.** If the PDF contains vector text and graphics, those can render cleanly into an image. If the PDF is itself a scan of a paper document, the quality ceiling is set by the quality of the original scan, not by the conversion step.

**How the PDF was built.** A document designed for print typically contains more visual detail than one optimized for on-screen use or email. That extra detail comes through in the converted image.

**How you plan to display the image.** A JPG that looks sharp on a monitor at its natural pixel size may show visible compression artifacts when you scale it up or print it large. If you need the image for print, check whether the original PDF was created at print resolution before converting.

One thing worth knowing: [PDF/A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF/A) is an archival standard that guarantees a document stays readable and reproducible far into the future. Converting a PDF/A file to JPG drops that guarantee, since JPG is not an archival format. For long-term record keeping, consider keeping the original PDF alongside any images you create.

## When a PDF is still the better choice

Converting makes sense for a specific set of jobs. A PDF stays the right format when:

- You need the text to be selectable, copyable, and searchable. JPG images contain no readable text; they contain pixels that happen to look like text.
- The document needs to look identical across different printers and screen sizes. [PDF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF) is designed for device-independent rendering, which is why legal and financial documents default to it.
- You need to keep interactive elements: form fields, clickable links, or bookmarks. Those disappear when you convert to an image.
- File size matters. A 10-page PDF converted to 10 individual JPGs will often take up more total disk space than the original single file.

If any of those apply, keep the PDF.
