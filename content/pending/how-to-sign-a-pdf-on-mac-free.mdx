## Sign a PDF on Mac for free, right now

The fastest free way to sign a PDF on a Mac is [Preview](https://support.apple.com/guide/preview/fill-out-and-sign-pdf-forms-prvw35725/mac), Apple's built-in PDF viewer. No account, no installation, no upload. Preview has carried a signature tool since macOS Mountain Lion and it works on every Mac you're likely to be using today.

If you're on a borrowed Mac or a school computer where you'd rather not save files locally, the browser-based [Sign PDF tool](/sign-pdf) is a solid second option. It runs entirely in the browser and your file never leaves your machine.

## How to sign a PDF in Preview, step by step

1. In Finder, right-click the PDF and choose **Open With > Preview**. If Preview is already your default PDF viewer, just double-click the file.
2. Click the **Markup** toolbar button near the top right of the window. It looks like a pencil tip. If you don't see it, go to **View > Show Markup Toolbar**.
3. Click the **Signature** button (the icon that looks like a handwritten name). If you haven't saved a signature yet, Preview prompts you to create one now.
4. Click your saved signature in the dropdown to place it on the page.
5. Drag it to the right spot. Pull a corner handle to resize it.
6. Choose **File > Export as PDF** to save. See the note below about why Export works better than Command + S for signed documents.

## Three ways to create your signature

Preview gives you three methods to capture a signature. Try all three if you're not happy with the first result.

**Trackpad.** Click Trackpad when the signature panel opens. Place your finger on the trackpad when prompted, sign your name slowly, then press any key. The result can look a bit shaky at first. Going slowly helps, and a fine-tipped stylus (if you have one) makes a big difference.

**Built-in camera.** Click Camera. Sign your name in dark ink on white paper, then hold it up to your Mac's camera, centered on the blue line. Preview captures it after a couple of seconds. This method usually produces the cleanest result because you can spend time on the paper signature before capturing it.

**iPhone or iPad.** If your iPhone or iPad is nearby and signed into the same Apple ID, Preview shows a third option to use the device. Tap the notification on your phone, sign with your finger or an Apple Pencil on iPad, and tap Done. The signature transfers to Preview automatically. This option requires macOS Ventura or later and a compatible iPhone or iPad.

Once you've created a signature, Preview saves it and shows it in the dropdown every time you click the Signature tool. You can store several signatures (useful if you need both a full signature and initials) and pick the right one each time.

## Why your signature might not be saving

The most common frustration with Preview signing is opening the document again and finding the signature gone. There are two usual causes.

**Use Export, not Save.** Pressing Command + S in Preview writes a copy of the file, but signature annotations are not always flattened into the PDF content this way. The annotation can vanish when another app opens the file. To avoid this, choose **File > Export as PDF** after signing, save it as a new file, and then open that file to confirm the signature is visible before sending it.

**Signing from a temporary location.** If you open a PDF directly from Mail or a browser download without saving it to your Mac first, Preview is working on a temp copy. When you close the window, the changes go nowhere. The fix is to save or download the file to your Desktop or Documents folder first, sign there, then Export as PDF.

## Is a Preview signature legally valid?

For most everyday contracts, lease agreements, and business documents, yes. The [US ESIGN Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/106th-congress/senate-bill/761) (enacted in 2000) and similar laws in most other countries make electronic signatures as enforceable as handwritten ones. A signature drawn in Preview counts as an electronic signature under that framework.

There are exceptions. Some government forms require a wet ink signature. Notarized documents, wills, and certain court filings in some jurisdictions have their own rules. If the document you're signing came with instructions, follow those, and check with the sender if you're unsure.

One more distinction worth knowing: Preview creates an [electronic signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_signature) but not a cryptographic digital signature. A cryptographic digital signature embeds a certificate that proves who signed the document and whether it was changed afterwards. Preview does not do that. For high-stakes documents where an audit trail matters (employment contracts, financial agreements, legal settlements), a dedicated e-signature service that issues signing certificates is the appropriate tool. For everything else, Preview is sufficient.
