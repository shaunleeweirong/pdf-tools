You can compress a PDF for Google Classroom using the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf), free and in your browser. Upload your file, pick a compression level, and download the smaller version in seconds. The key thing to know before you do: compression helps image-heavy PDFs like scanned homework, but it makes typed or printed PDFs noticeably larger.

## Why are PDFs so large in Google Classroom?

The most common culprit is scanning. When you photograph handwritten homework with your phone or use a scanner app, each page becomes a high-resolution JPEG image embedded in a PDF file. A single scanned page can weigh 1 to 3 MB at full resolution. A five-page assignment, then, arrives at 5 to 15 MB or more.

That's fine for archiving, but it creates friction in Google Classroom. Large files take longer to upload on shared school Wi-Fi, longer to open on a teacher's phone, and longer to review during class. Some school network proxies also time out on slow uploads, which can make it look like the file failed to submit.

Teachers who upload worksheets or resource packets with lots of embedded diagrams or photos run into the same issue.

## Does Google Classroom have a file size limit?

Not a restrictive one for PDFs. [Google Drive](https://support.google.com/drive/answer/37603), which stores all Classroom files, allows individual files up to 5 TB. So a large PDF won't be rejected outright.

The practical problem is speed. A 20 MB PDF attached to an assignment is slow to download for every student in the class, especially on mobile data or a congested school Wi-Fi network. Keeping submitted PDFs under 5 MB, and shared resources under 2 MB, makes the Classroom experience noticeably smoother.

## When compression actually helps

Compression makes sense when your PDF is made up mostly of images. Scanned handwritten homework, worksheets with photos, presentation slides saved as PDFs, and receipts photographed on a phone all fall into this category. The compressor converts each page to a JPEG, which trims the file size significantly when the content is already raster-based.

Our compress tool offers three levels:

- **Low (best quality):** small reduction, clear text and images
- **Medium:** good balance of size and quality, the right starting point for most scanned homework
- **High (smallest file):** biggest reduction, some visible quality drop at 100% zoom

For a multi-page image-heavy PDF, Medium and High settings can cut the file to a fraction of its original size.

## When compression makes files larger (and what to do instead)

If your PDF is a typed document, a printed form, a contract, or anything that started life as digital text, a rasterizing compressor will make it bigger. Sometimes much bigger. That's because a text PDF stores characters as vector data, which is compact. The compressor throws that away and replaces each page with a photo of the page instead.

In our own testing, a 20-page text report grew from 23.5 KB to 1.4 MB after compression. That's more than 60 times the original size. The same principle applies to typed homework submitted as a PDF, invoices, and any form filled and saved digitally.

For these files, the right move is to use the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) to break the document into smaller sections, or to share it directly as a Google Doc instead, which keeps the file size tiny.

## How to compress a scanned PDF for Google Classroom

1. Go to the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) on this site.
2. Click "Select PDF" or drag your file onto the page. Nothing is uploaded to a server; the work happens in your browser.
3. Pick a compression level. For a scanned homework submission, Medium is usually the right call. If the result is still too large, repeat from the original file at High.
4. Click Compress. Download the output file.
5. Upload the compressed file to your Google Classroom assignment.

The whole process takes under a minute for most files.

One thing to avoid: don't compress the same file twice hoping to squeeze more out. Running the output through compression again at the same level makes the file larger, not smaller, because re-encoding an already-compressed JPEG adds data rather than removing it. If Medium didn't get the file small enough, start from your original and try High instead.

## Tips for Chromebook users

Chromebooks are common in K-12 classrooms, and browser-based compression tools are the simplest option here since there's nothing to install. Open the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) in Chrome, upload your file, compress it, and save the result to your Downloads folder or directly to Google Drive. The tool uses [WebAssembly](https://webassembly.org/) to run the compression locally in your browser tab.

If you have a lot of scanned pages and the total file is still large after compression, consider splitting the submission into two parts using the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf). This is common for long lab reports or multi-page handwritten assignments.

## Why your scan settings matter for next time

If you're using your phone to scan homework, the [resolution setting in your scan app](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_resolution) makes a big difference before any compression happens. Scanning at 150 to 200 DPI in grayscale keeps file sizes reasonable without sacrificing readability. Most scanner apps default to a much higher resolution aimed at archival quality, which isn't needed for a homework submission.

Adjusting the scan settings at the start can get you a clean, legible PDF under 1 MB per page, without needing any compression step at all.
