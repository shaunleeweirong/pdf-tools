Compressing a PDF before uploading to Google Drive can cut an image-heavy file by 65% or more, freeing up quota and making your shared links load faster. The catch: it only works on the right kind of PDF. Here's what you need to know before you run anything through a compressor.

## Why Google Drive storage fills up faster than you'd expect

[Google Drive gives free accounts 15 GB](https://support.google.com/drive/answer/19015), split across Drive, Gmail, and Google Photos. PDFs count at their full file size. A single presentation-heavy PDF from a vendor, a set of scanned invoices, or a high-resolution photo book can chew through several gigabytes in one go.

Google Drive doesn't compress PDFs when you upload them. What you put in is what gets stored. So if you've got a 40 MB PDF sitting in your Drive, it's using 40 MB of your quota.

Smaller files also share better. When you send someone a Drive link to a large PDF, they have to wait for it to load in the browser. Cut the file to a third of its original size and the page opens near-instantly.

## Which PDFs actually shrink when you compress them

This is the part most guides skip.

Our [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) rasterizes every page to JPEG. That approach works well when a page is already an image, because JPEG can re-encode a photograph at a lower quality and lose relatively little. It doesn't work when a page is text, because text stored as PDF vector glyphs is tiny. Converting those glyphs to a photograph of glyphs makes the file much, much bigger.

Here's what that looks like with real numbers from our own testing:

| PDF type | Before | After (medium) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Image deck (6 pages, full-bleed photos) | 913.4 KB | 315.3 KB | -65.5% |
| Text report (20 pages, body text only) | 23.5 KB | 1,400.8 KB | +5861% |

A six-page image deck shrank by nearly two-thirds. A twenty-page text report got sixty times bigger.

**Compress if your PDF is:** scanned pages, photo books, slide decks where each slide is a full image, brochures heavy on photos.

**Skip compression if your PDF is:** a report, a contract, an invoice, a form, or anything else where most of the content is text. You'll make it worse.

## How to compress a PDF for Google Drive, step by step

1. Open the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) in your browser. No sign-up needed, no app to install.

5. Check the file size. If it's smaller than the original, upload it to Google Drive. If it's bigger, your PDF is probably text-heavy and compression isn't the right fix.

## Which compression level to pick

The tool offers three levels:

| Level | Image deck result | Reduction |
|---|---|---|
| Low (best quality) | 703.5 KB | -23.0% |
| Medium | 315.3 KB | -65.5% |
| High (smallest file) | 175.6 KB | -80.8% |

Medium is a good starting point for most scanned or image-heavy PDFs. High gets you the smallest file but with a visible quality drop on dense image pages. Low is worth trying if the file doesn't need to be tiny and you want to keep it looking sharp.

Start from the original file each time. If medium isn't small enough, start over from the original at high, rather than running medium again on the already-compressed output.

## What if compressing makes your PDF larger

That's a clear sign the PDF is mostly text. Don't run it through a second time hoping for a different result. Compressing an already-compressed image PDF at medium quality grew the file by 90% in our testing. A second pass on a text PDF would push it even further in the wrong direction.

For text-heavy PDFs that are taking up too much storage, a few other approaches work better:

- **Split the file.** Use the [Split PDF tool](/split-pdf) to break a long document into sections. Upload only the parts you actually need in Drive.
- **Remove pages you don't need.** The [Delete Pages tool](/delete-pages) lets you cut out blank pages, cover pages, or appendices before uploading.
- **Check for embedded fonts and revision history.** A text PDF that's unexpectedly large often has a full revision history embedded, or very large fonts. Adobe Acrobat's "Optimize PDF" feature can strip those. Our browser tool doesn't strip fonts.

## Does Google Drive compress PDFs when you open them?

One method you'll sometimes see suggested: right-click a PDF in Drive, open it with Google Docs, then re-download it as a PDF. Google's conversion can sometimes reduce the file size for text-heavy PDFs by stripping embedded fonts. But it also tends to shift formatting, reflow text, and alter how tables and columns look. It's worth trying on a copy, but check the result carefully before relying on it.

If your PDF has forms, signatures, or precise layout that needs to stay intact, this method will almost certainly break something.

## Keeping your Drive quota under control

Once you've compressed and uploaded your PDF, it's worth doing a quick audit. In Google Drive, you can see your storage usage broken down by file type. Large PDFs are often the culprit. [Google's storage management page](https://drive.google.com/settings/storage) shows your biggest files sorted by size, which makes it easy to spot what's worth compressing or deleting.

The [PDF format itself](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PDF) supports internal compression of image streams, fonts, and content, but how much compression any given tool applies depends entirely on its implementation. Browser-based tools like ours apply JPEG re-encoding to every page, which is why they're effective on image content and counterproductive on text.
