## How to protect a PDF from copying for free

The most effective free way to protect a PDF from copying is to [password-protect the whole file](/protect-pdf). If no one can open it without a password, there's nothing to copy. For files you need to share publicly, adding a [watermark](/watermark-pdf) is the practical alternative: it won't block copying, but it deters casual sharing and makes the source traceable.

Neither approach is foolproof, and understanding why matters before you pick one.

## Two things people mean by "copy protection"

When someone asks how to protect a PDF from copying, they usually mean one of two different things:

**Access control.** You want to limit who can open the file. The solution is a password: without it, the PDF won't open in any standard reader, and the content is completely inaccessible.

**Content restrictions.** You want people to read the file, but not select and copy the text. The [PDF format](https://pdfa.org/top-three-ways-to-improve-your-pdf-document-security/) includes "permission flags" designed to do exactly this.

These two things are not the same, and the second one is a lot weaker in practice than it sounds. Access control is the only approach that reliably delivers on what the name implies.

## Method 1: Password-protect the file (strongest option)

Encrypting a PDF with a password is the cleanest free approach. Our [Protect PDF tool](/protect-pdf) does this entirely in your browser using [AES-256 encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Encryption_Standard), the same standard used for government and financial data. Your file never leaves your device.

Here's how to do it:

1. Open the [Protect PDF tool](/protect-pdf).
2. Drop your PDF onto the page.
3. Type your password into the field.
4. Click Protect and download the encrypted file.

Anyone who receives the file needs the correct password to open it. Without it, the content is completely locked. No text to select, no pages to print, no content to copy.

The trade-off is straightforward: once you share the password, the recipient has full access. They can copy text, print, and forward both the file and the password to others. Password protection is gating, not DRM. It controls who gets in, not what they do once they're in.

## Method 2: Add a watermark (best for shared files)

If you need to share a PDF openly and want some protection against the content being circulated without credit, a visible watermark is practical. Our [Watermark PDF tool](/watermark-pdf) stamps custom text across every page of your PDF in the browser.

You choose the text: your name, "CONFIDENTIAL", a company name, a date. It appears across all pages, clearly visible.

A watermark doesn't technically block anything. Text is still selectable, the file is still shareable. What it does is make copying obvious and create a clear record of the source. If someone shares your watermarked PDF without permission, the mark goes with it. That's usually enough to stop casual redistribution.

## Why PDF permission flags aren't reliable

The PDF specification (ISO 32000) includes an "owner password" feature that lets you set restrictions on what recipients can do with a file: allow reading but not printing, or allow printing but not copying text.

In practice, this protection is weak:

- Many free PDF readers do not enforce permission flags consistently.
- Free online tools can strip permission flags from any PDF in seconds.
- Anyone who can read the page can take a screenshot of it.

Permission flags were designed for controlled environments where specific software is required to open files. On the open web, where people can use dozens of different PDF readers and online tools, they provide friction but not real security.

If your goal is to make copy-pasting slightly inconvenient for the average user, permissions flags achieve that. If your goal is to prevent determined copying, they don't.

## Which method fits your situation

A quick guide based on what you're trying to do:

**You want to control who can see the file.** Use [password protection](/protect-pdf). No password, no access, no copying. This is the strongest free option.

**You're sharing the file openly but want to discourage redistribution.** Use a [watermark](/watermark-pdf). It's a visible deterrent and makes the source identifiable if the file spreads.

**You need both.** Protect the file with a password and also add a watermark, so even recipients with the password have a visible reminder that the content isn't for redistribution.

**You need true copy prevention with verified access control.** There's no free way to do this reliably. Paid DRM systems can lock documents to specific accounts or devices, but they're expensive, add friction for readers, and still can't stop a camera pointed at a screen.

For most real-world cases, a password plus a watermark covers the risk: the password blocks unauthorized access, the watermark discourages authorized recipients from casually forwarding the content.

## What no free tool can stop

Any content you can see, you can copy. A screenshot takes what's on the screen. A phone camera captures a printed page. PDF protection works at the file level, not at the perception level.

Set realistic expectations: you can prevent unauthorized access (passwords), make copying inconvenient and obvious (watermarks + permission flags), and create a clear record if content leaks (watermarks). You can't make digital content uncopyable.

For protecting sensitive documents, those three things together are usually more than enough.
