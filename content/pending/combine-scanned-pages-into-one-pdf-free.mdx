Combining scanned pages into one PDF is a two-step job: convert your scans to PDF if they're not already, then merge them into a single file. Both steps are free and run entirely in your browser using the [Images to PDF](/jpg-to-pdf) and [Merge PDF](/merge-pdf) tools.

## Which situation are you in?

The right approach depends on what your scanner saved:

- **Image files (JPG, PNG):** Your scanner saved each page as a separate photo. You need to convert those images to PDF and combine them in one pass.
- **Separate PDF files:** Your scanner or app already saved each page as its own PDF. You just need to merge them.

Both take under a minute.

## Step by step: combining image scans (JPG, PNG)

1. Open the [Images to PDF tool](/jpg-to-pdf).
2. Click "Select files" and choose all your scanned images at once, or drag and drop them into the drop zone.
3. Drag the thumbnails to arrange the pages in the right order.
4. Click "Convert" and download your combined PDF.

The images are converted and packaged into a single PDF in one pass. You don't need to convert each image separately before merging.

## Step by step: combining scanned PDFs

If each page is already its own PDF file, skip conversion and go straight to the [Merge PDF tool](/merge-pdf).

1. Open [Merge PDF](/merge-pdf).
2. Upload all the individual PDF files.
3. Drag the tiles to put them in the correct order.
4. Click "Merge" and save the combined file.

Merging does not bloat the result. In testing, three files totalling 971.9 KB merged to a 970.5 KB output, a difference of 0.1%. Combining your pages won't push the total over an email limit.

## Your files stay on your device

Both tools run in your browser. Nothing is sent to any server. This matters for scanned documents, which often contain contracts, medical records, tax forms, or other private material you wouldn't want sitting on a third-party server. The [PDF format](https://www.pdfa.org/resource/iso-32000-pdf/) is an open ISO standard, and modern browsers handle it well enough that a cloud round-trip is simply not needed here.

## What if the pages are in the wrong order?

Both tools let you reorder before finalizing. In the Images to PDF tool, drag the image thumbnails into the right sequence before you click Convert. In the Merge PDF tool, drag the PDF tiles to reorder them.

If you download the result and notice the order is wrong, use the [Organize PDF tool](/organize-pdf). It shows a visual grid of all your pages and lets you drag them into any arrangement before re-exporting.

## Phone scans: same process, in a mobile browser

If you scanned with your phone's camera or a scanning app, each page typically saves as a JPG or a single-page PDF. The same steps work fine on a phone browser.

On iPhone, the Files app and Notes both have built-in document scanners. Each scan saves as a separate file. Open Safari, go to the Images to PDF tool, and add the files from your Files app.

On Android, Google Drive's scanner and Google Lens save pages individually as PDFs or images. Open Chrome, go to the Merge PDF or Images to PDF tool, and follow the same steps above.

## Why does the combined PDF look blurry?

If the images looked fine on screen but the PDF looks soft when printed, the issue is scan resolution, not the combining step. Most quick scans are done at 150 dpi, which is fine for reading on screen but soft in print. For anything you plan to print, scan at 300 dpi. The tools embed images at whatever resolution they arrive with; they don't downsample.

According to [Adobe's scanning guide](https://www.adobe.com/acrobat/hub/how-to/scan-to-pdf.html), 300 dpi is the standard recommendation for printed documents, while 150 dpi is acceptable for purely digital use.

## Does combining make the file too large?

It can, if the scans themselves are large. Combining doesn't add much overhead, but a stack of 300 dpi scans will naturally produce a big PDF.

If the result is too large to send by email or upload somewhere, the [Compress PDF tool](/compress-pdf) can help, but only if the PDF is mostly photographic content. A 6-page image PDF dropped from 913.4 KB to 175.6 KB at the highest compression setting. That's meaningful.

There's a catch, though: the compressor rasterizes every page to JPEG. It works well on image scans, but it will make a text-heavy PDF much larger, not smaller. If your scanned pages are printed text rather than photos, compression will hurt you. In that case, splitting the document or zipping it are better options for managing size.

[Gmail](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/6584) and Outlook both cap attachments at 25 MB. Because email encoding adds roughly a third to the file size, the practical ceiling for an emailed PDF is around 18 MB.
